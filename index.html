The Simulator

Drag the dials

A company beats on revenue and the stock falls nine percent. Everyone finds this insane. It isn't.

Markets don't pay for the quarter that happened. They pay for the quarter everyone now expects to happen next. So a small cut to forward guidance can bury a genuinely good three months — and usually does.

Move the dials and watch which one actually swings the number. It's not the one on the front page of the earnings release.

Revenue surprise+3.0%
How much this quarter's sales beat or missed what analysts expected.
Guidance revision−2.0%
What the company now says about next quarter, against what the market had penciled in.
Multiple re-rating0.0%
Whether investors are willing to pay more or less for each dollar of profit — the mood, essentially.

−8.6%

Revenue beat +2.4
Guidance −11.0
Re-rating 0.0
Guidance is weighted far more heavily than the quarter itself. That's the entire trick — and it's why "beat and fell" headlines aren't a contradiction.

This is a teaching model, not a forecast. Real reactions depend on positioning, sector, rates and what management said on the call. The weightings here are illustrative and deliberately simple — the point is the relative size of the levers, not a number to trade on.

Every issue takes one of these and does it properly. Once a week.

Terminal Value Issue № 001

The quarter was fine. The guidance was the story.

The move

Revenue beat. The stock fell nine percent anyway. That gap between "good quarter" and "bad day" is where most of the actual information lives.

The number

Next quarter's guide came in two percent under consensus. Two percent. That was the entire nine.

Worth keeping

Markets don't price what happened. They price the revision to what they expected to happen.

Why this exists

About

Terminal value is what a business is worth after the forecast runs out — the part that's left when the noise stops.

That's the job here. Markets generate an enormous amount of noise every single day, almost none of which survives contact with the following Tuesday. This column ignores nearly all of it and writes about the small number of things that will still matter in a year.

No predictions. No tickers to buy. No breathless thread about a company we'd never heard of last week. Just one thing that moved, explained properly, by someone doing the reading so you don't have to.

It's written for the person who already knows what a bond is, follows the market on and off, and is quietly tired of choosing between coverage that assumes you know nothing and coverage that assumes you do this for a living.
