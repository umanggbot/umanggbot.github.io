The One-Man Fed

Umang  ·  26 Jul 2026  ·  10 min read

July 26, 2026 (Sunday special edition)

The Federal Reserve's rate-setting committee meets on Tuesday and Wednesday: nineteen officials, hundreds of PhD economists in support, a communications apparatus that weighs adverbs like plutonium. Ahead of that, I want to spend today on some history, because the Fed is young enough that you can meet the thing it replaced. The thing it replaced was one enormous, terrifying old man with a diseased nose, and the week he spent saving the American financial system is still the best crash course in what central banking actually is.

The library

Saturday night, November 2, 1907. New York has been in full financial panic for two weeks. The stock market has fallen by nearly half from its peak the year before. Depositors are queuing overnight outside trust companies, the era's shadow banks. Call-money rates on the exchange floor have touched 100 percent annualized. There is no central bank; America abolished its last one in 1836, on the theory that concentrated financial power was un-American, and has since run the fastest-growing economy on earth with no lender of last resort, no deposit insurance, and a currency supply with the flexibility of a cast-iron pipe.

What America has instead, at this particular moment, is John Pierpont Morgan, age 70, half-retired, summoned back from an Episcopal church convention in Richmond when the trouble started.

That Saturday night, Morgan gathers roughly fifty presidents of the city's trust companies in his private library on East 36th Street, marble, tapestries, Gutenberg Bibles, and tells them, in substance, that they will pool $25 million to save the weakest among them, because if they do not, Monday will destroy them all. The negotiations crawl. Sometime after midnight, Morgan closes the library door and puts the key in his pocket. Nobody leaves until the paper is signed. At 4:45 a.m., the senior trust president signs, and the rest follow.

Everyone knows that image; it's the most famous door in financial history. The part people skip is what happened before Morgan locked it, and the before is where all the actual content lives.

Two weeks earlier, when the panic first hit, the natural move was to save the first domino: the Knickerbocker Trust, third-largest in the city, run by a Morgan acquaintance, wobbling after its president was linked to a failed scheme to corner the copper market. Everyone begged Morgan to rescue it. He sent a young man named Benjamin Strong to go through Knickerbocker's books overnight, and when Strong reported that he could not certify the thing as solvent, Morgan let it die. It failed on October 22. Then the mob turned to the Trust Company of America, and Strong's examiners went through those books, and this time the answer was different: illiquid, yes; insolvent, no. Morgan's verdict, as recorded: then this is the place to stop the trouble.

Hold that pair of decisions in your mind. One institution allowed to fail, one saved, on the basis of an overnight forensic distinction between broke and merely busy. That distinction is the entire intellectual content of central banking. Everything since is elaboration.

The rest of the fortnight is a highlight reel of improvised authority. The city of New York, days from missing payroll: Morgan takes $30 million of its bonds. The stock exchange, about to close early because no one will lend against stocks: Morgan summons the bank presidents at 2 p.m. and raises $25 million in about ten minutes, and the exchange stays open. A Sunday comes, and he asks the city's clergy to preach calm; the sermons are, in effect, part of the financial plumbing that weekend. John D. Rockefeller deposits $10 million into the pool and tells reporters he'd give half of all he had to maintain America's credit. The US Treasury, for its part, deposits $35 million into national banks and then is tapped out; the Secretary of the Treasury essentially hands Morgan the keys and watches.

Note what Morgan is doing in functional terms. He is inventing, live, with his own and borrowed money, the machinery every modern crisis manager now runs by statute: emergency liquidity against collateral, triage between solvent and insolvent, coordination of private balance sheets, communication designed to break the psychology of the run. Walter Bagehot had written the recipe in 1873 (in a panic, lend freely, on good collateral, at a penalty rate), but Bagehot assumed a central bank would do it. In America the central bank was one mortal man with gout, working from a smoke-filled library, sustained on cigars and, during the worst nights, almost no sleep.

Why did it work? Because a panic, at its mechanical heart, is an information vacuum. Every bank borrows short and lends long, so no bank can survive all its depositors at once, so the only thing standing between any bank and death is the depositors' belief that other depositors aren't coming. In the vacuum, the rational move is to run first and ask later, and everyone runs. What stops it is a credible entity that can see inside the balance sheets and is willing to bet its own money on the difference between illiquid and insolvent. The Fed does this today with examiners and discount windows. In 1907 the credible entity was Morgan's eyes plus Strong's ledgers plus a reputation so heavy that his yes or no functioned as a clearing price. People trusted the verdict because the verdict was collateralized by Morgan's entire life.

The work

That reputation was not conjured for the occasion, and the résumé behind it is worth the tour, because Morgan's whole career was a single idea applied at increasing scale. The idea: unmanaged competition and unmanaged panic were the same disease (chaos, expensive to everyone) and his job was to impose order on both, whether or not anyone had asked him to.

He was born in Hartford in 1837, son of a transatlantic banker who drilled into him that a banker's capital is his good name. He spent a sickly childhood in and out of convalescence, and grew into a physique that did half his negotiating: over six feet, barrel-chested, with a rhinophyma-swollen nose he refused to fix and eyes that the photographer Edward Steichen, who shot the famous 1903 portrait where the chair's armrest gleams like a dagger, said were like staring into the headlights of an oncoming express train.

The signature work was the railroads. American railways in the Gilded Age were finance's Wild West: overbuilt, over-leveraged, run by buccaneers, permanently at war over rates, and serially bankrupt; by the mid-1890s something like a third of the nation's track mileage had passed through receivership. Morgan's firm made itself the emergency room. He would take a broken railroad, force the old shareholders to accept losses, cut the debt to what the traffic could actually pay, install his own men on the board through voting trusts, and end the rate wars by the simple expedient of making former combatants share directors. The press called it Morganization. It was the invention of modern restructuring, and also, less charmingly, the invention of engineering-away-competition, and both descriptions are accurate at once, which is the thing about Morgan you have to be able to hold.

The state, in this period, kept discovering that it needed him. In 1895 the US Treasury's gold reserve, the thing that made dollars credibly convertible, was draining toward nothing (below the statutory $100 million floor, heading for the point where the government literally could not pay in gold). President Cleveland, out of options, took a deal from Morgan and the Rothschilds' American agent: a private syndicate would deliver 3.5 million ounces of gold in exchange for bonds, executed through a forgotten Civil War statute Morgan himself dug up and put on the table. The syndicate propped the dollar, turned a profit doing it, and Congress spent years investigating; Morgan, asked at a hearing what he had expected to make, answered that he had been trying to save the country's credit and had not thought it his business to calculate. Believe him or don't. The gold arrived.

And in 1901 came the deal that still defines scale. Andrew Carnegie, ready to retire, scribbled his price on a slip of paper; Morgan glanced at it and said he accepted. The figure was $480 million for Carnegie Steel, and around it Morgan assembled United States Steel, capitalized at $1.4 billion: the first billion-dollar corporation, in an economy whose entire annual output was around $20 billion. As a share of the economy, imagine a single new company today capitalized somewhere north of a trillion and a half dollars, formed over a few weeks of meetings. When Morgan congratulated Carnegie on becoming the richest man in the world, he was, characteristically, announcing the score of a game he had just redesigned.

Character

The republic never decided how it felt about him, and the ambivalence was earned from both directions. The same man who stopped the 1907 panic used the panic to let U.S. Steel swallow a major Southern competitor, with a president's antitrust blessing extracted overnight while the market hung hostage. The same public that cheered the rescue then spent 1912 watching the Pujo Committee map the "money trust": Morgan partners and allies holding directorships across banks, railroads, and industrials that added up, by the committee's count, to influence over resources exceeding $22 billion, a number chosen to frighten and successful at it. The hearings produced the exchange he is remembered by. Asked by counsel whether commercial credit was based primarily upon money or property, Morgan answered: no, sir; the first thing is character. Money cannot buy it, he went on, because a man he did not trust could not get money from him on all the bonds in Christendom.

Cynics note that the man who said this had spent a career deciding, personally, who had character, with his own firm frequently on the profitable end of the verdict. Also true. But 1907 is the empirical test of whether the line was mere pose, and it wasn't: the entire rescue ran on exactly that faculty. Strong's ledgers could establish solvency. Only credibility could make fifty frightened trust presidents accept the finding at 4 a.m. and wire the money by Monday. The panic ended when Morgan's judgment became, briefly, the currency everyone agreed to clear in. That is what a lender of last resort is: an entity whose balance sheet and verdicts are trusted when nothing else is. In 1907, the balance sheet was adequate. The verdicts were the whole game.

He died sixteen months after Pujo, in his sleep at the Grand Hotel in Rome, on March 31, 1913, age 75. Wall Street flew flags at half-mast; the exchange he had kept open paused trading in his honor. His estate came to about $68 million outside the art, prompting Rockefeller's immortal, backhanded eulogy: and to think, he wasn't even a rich man.

Nine months later, in December 1913, Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act. The line from the library to the Act is direct and documented: the men who drafted the blueprint at their secret Jekyll Island retreat in 1910 included Morgan partners and allies, and the first head of the New York Fed, the operating heart of the new system, was Benjamin Strong, the same man who had gone through Knickerbocker's books overnight. America looked at the 1907 rescue, understood with perfect clarity that it had outsourced its monetary survival to the mortality and goodwill of one man, and built a bureaucracy to replace him. The one-man Fed became the Fed.

Replace, at least, in the org chart. Here is the thought I'd leave you with before Wednesday's meeting, and before an autumn that (between gated credit funds, a bond market asking fiscal questions, and a war with a dead ceasefire) may yet ask the old question again. Every acute crisis since 1913 has, at its worst hour, collapsed back into the library: a weekend, closed doors, a handful of people deciding which institutions are illiquid and which are insolvent, with imperfect ledgers and no time. Bear Stearns and Lehman were decided in rooms like that in 2008, one saved-ish, one let go, by officials openly haunted by which was which. The 2023 bank failures were resolved over a weekend of balance-sheet triage that Benjamin Strong would have recognized instantly, down to the overnight hours. We built the committee, the models, the statutes, and the acronyms precisely so that no one would ever again have to trust one man's eyes. And at every true extremity, what the system still runs on is some person, in some room, making Morgan's call with Morgan's information, minus the cigar.

The committee meets Wednesday. It will manage, with nineteen votes and four hundred economists, the ordinary weather. It exists because of the storms, and the storms have never once read the org chart.
