Gasoline Is Doing All the Talking

Umang  ·  12 Aug 2026  ·  7 min read

The government reported this morning that inflation is running at 3.4 percent.

The government reported this morning that inflation is running at 3.4 percent. Your grocery bill disagrees; food is up about 3. Your rent disagrees in the other direction if you signed recently; new leases have gone quiet. And the gas station down the street is laughing at all of it, because gasoline is up 24.6 percent from a year ago, and if you drive for a living you have spent 2026 wondering what planet the 3.4 percent people live on.

Everyone in that paragraph is right. That's the thing about inflation numbers that almost no one says plainly: the CPI is an average of wildly different experiences, and this particular 3.4 percent is one of the strangest composites in years, a number that is simultaneously worse than it looks, better than it looks, and mostly not about what people think it's about. Today I want to take the thing apart on the table, piece by piece, because July's report is the clearest demonstration in a long time of the most useful skill in economic literacy: never accept an inflation number until you've seen its ingredients.

The cart and the memory

Two pieces of machinery first, quickly, because everything else depends on them.

The CPI is a shopping cart. The government tracks the prices of a vast basket weighted to what the average urban household buys: roughly a third housing, then transport, food, medical care, and down the long tail to haircuts and funerals. Nobody actually owns this cart. If you rent and commute by car, your personal inflation is worse than headline right now; if you own your home outright and walk to work, better. The weights hide one glorious oddity worth knowing: since most Americans own rather than rent, the single biggest line in the index is "owners' equivalent rent," the amount homeowners tell surveyors they would charge themselves to rent their own house. The largest component of the most important number in economics is a thought experiment. It's a defensible thought experiment (housing services are real consumption), but when someone quotes CPI to a decimal place with moral certainty, remember that a third of it is Americans estimating their own hypothetical rent.

Second: "3.4 percent" is a memory, not a reading. It compares today's cart to the same cart twelve months ago, which means it contains everything that happened in between, including things that already stopped happening. The fresher information is the monthly change, and July's was 0.1 percent, which annualizes to barely over 1. By the month, this was a soft report, a quiet summer for prices. The 3.4 is dragging a year of history behind it, and most of that history has a specific name, which brings us to the ingredients.

The relay

Pull the report apart and it splits into two economies moving at completely different speeds.

Speed one: almost everything. Core inflation (excluding food and energy) is at 2.5 percent annually and fell again this month. Core goods (the stuff in containers: clothes, appliances, furniture) are up less than 1 percent on the year. Used cars are cheaper than last year. Shelter, the great post-pandemic villain, has cooled to 3.2 and keeps grinding down as old leases roll onto the quieter new-lease market. Medical drugs are outright deflating. Hotels fell in July, in a summer when the country hosted the World Cup and every hospitality analyst predicted gouging; the gouging capacity apparently wasn't there. If you handed a central banker this half of the report cold, they'd say: two-point-something, converging, close the folder and go to lunch.

Speed two: energy. Gasoline up 24.6 percent year over year. Overall energy up 14.7. Airfares up 25.5 percent on the year (jet fuel is most of a ticket's marginal cost). And a slow seep, documented in the wonkier analyses, of energy costs into trucking, packaging, and delivered-goods prices, the way diesel always propagates: quietly, with a lag, into everything that moves.

Now do the attribution honestly. What is the 3.4 percent? It is roughly 2.5 points of ordinary, cooling, post-pandemic inflation, plus roughly a point of war premium, concentrated brutally in fuel. Strip the energy complex and America's inflation problem in August 2026 looks close to solved. Include it and we're a full point and a half above target for a fifth straight year.

Here's the part that turns this from a data note into an argument. This decomposition is completely different from last year's, which was completely different from the year before's, and I think the least appreciated fact about the entire 2021-2026 inflation era is that it has been a relay race: a sequence of different inflations wearing the same number. Leg one, 2021-22: goods, ports, semiconductors, stimulus checks chasing sofas. Leg two, 2023-24: services and shelter, as wages and rents caught up. Leg three, 2025: tariffs, an engineered import-price shock. Leg four, 2026: war energy. Each leg handed the baton to the next just as it faded, and headline inflation never got to touch 2, so it reads in the history books as one long five-year failure. It was four different events, with four different causes, requiring four different responses, filed under one word.

And the tariff leg deserves its own paragraph, because 2026 ran a natural experiment nobody designed. In February the Supreme Court struck the tariffs down; by summer the government was refunding the money (June's refund checks exceeded $49 billion, an essay unto itself, which I wrote three weeks ago). The people who blamed the 2025 inflation uptick on tariffs predicted relief as the tariff effect washed out. The people who called tariffs a one-time level shift predicted little. The verdict is now in the data, and it's a split decision: core goods inflation did fade, to under 1 percent, right on the wash-out schedule. Headline didn't budge, because the war walked in as the tariffs walked out. Fighters in last year's tariff-inflation debate are both claiming vindication, and both halves are right, which is the relay race in miniature: the baton changed hands mid-argument.

Why does the attribution matter beyond scorekeeping? Because the treatment depends on the diagnosis, and the patient in the waiting room is the Federal Reserve.

If the residual inflation is demand (too much money chasing services), rate hikes are the tool, and the three dissenters from July 29 are right. If it's a war tax on fuel, hikes are approximately useless and actively cruel: the funds rate cannot refine a barrel of oil or sweep a mine out of the Strait of Hormuz, and hiking into a supply shock taxes Ohio for decisions made in Tehran. Add Friday's negative jobs number (minus 23,000, the freeze creaking) and the September meeting becomes the purest version of the central banker's nightmare: headline says tighten, core says done, payrolls say ease, and all three are the same economy.

My read, stated so you can grade it: this is 60-40 a supply-shock inflation that the Fed should look through, and the right September move is the hold they'll probably deliver, with the balance-sheet runoff doing the quiet tightening the new chair prefers anyway. The falsifiable part: if the Gulf war actually ends (the ceasefire's 60-day clock runs out this week, and the omens are bad) and headline hasn't converged most of the way to core within two quarters of a real peace, then I'm wrong, the sticky-3-percent camp wins, and the hikes should come. Watch the gap between headline and core; it is currently 0.9 points, it is almost entirely war, and its half-life is a military question, which is the most 2026 sentence I will write this month.

One last ingredient, the one no chart shows, because I think ignoring it is how economists earned the public's distrust of the whole subject. Inflation is a rate; pain is a level. When inflation "comes down" from 9 to 3.4, prices are not coming down; they are rising more slowly, permanently, from a base that never resets. The cart that cost $100 in early 2021 costs somewhere around $125 now, and 3.4 percent means it will cost $129 next year, and no future Fed triumph will ever make it cost $100 again. Disinflation is the fire spreading more slowly; the rooms already burnt stay burnt. Voters who fume about prices while the experts celebrate progress are not innumerate. They are measuring the level, because they live in the level, while the profession grades itself on the derivative. Five years into this, with gasoline underlining the point 24.6 percent in red, the gap between those two measurements has become a political fact as important as any monetary one.

The committee meets in five weeks with all three gauges disagreeing. Gasoline, as usual, will do most of the talking; the war will decide what it says.
