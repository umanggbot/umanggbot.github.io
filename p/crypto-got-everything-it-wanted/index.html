Crypto Got Everything It Wanted

On Thursday morning, the President of the United States stood with cryptocurrency executives and urged Congress to pass the Clarity Act, the industry's long-sought market-structure law. Bitcoin jumped 6.6 percent on the day, to $76,712.

That's the news. Here's the context the news never carries: a year ago bitcoin traded at $112,504. Its all-time high, $126,198, was set on October 6, 2025, ten months ago. Even after Thursday's pop, it is down 32 percent over twelve months and 39 percent from the peak; at its July low, near $61,900, it had been cut in half. Ethereum trades at $2,371, less than half its former glory. And this has happened during the most politically triumphant stretch in the industry's history: a strategic bitcoin reserve established by executive order, a stablecoin law signed, an SEC that dropped its enforcement siege, a White House that hosts crypto executives instead of subpoenaing them, and now a president personally whipping votes for the industry's bill.

Crypto spent fifteen years saying it would moon when the government finally got out of the way. The government got out of the way. It halved.

Today, for the final issue of this summer's run, I want to take that paradox seriously rather than as a punchline, because inside it is the cleanest lesson 2026 has taught about any asset: the difference between what a thing says it is and what it trades as. Bitcoin says it is digital gold, a hedge, an exit from the system. The tape says otherwise, and this year the tape has been unusually articulate.

The year the story got tested

Walk the timeline of the decline against the timeline of the victories, because the mismatch is the evidence.

The peak, October 2025, arrived with the political project already largely won: the reserve order signed months earlier, the stablecoin act celebrated in July, the Clarity Act through the House with a bipartisan vote. If legal legitimacy were the binding constraint on crypto's price, the constraint was gone before the top. What happened next tracked a different variable entirely. Over the winter and spring, as the new Fed chair arrived talking discipline, as real yields ground upward, as the long bond began its march toward 5.33 percent (Tuesday's essay), the most speculative corners of every market deflated in unison: unprofitable tech, meme stocks, AI infrastructure eventually, and crypto first and hardest, because crypto is the purest expression of the thing being repriced, which is the present value of stories.

Then the war. On July 8, with missiles in the air and oil up eight percent, bitcoin sat at $61,900, down with the Nasdaq, while its marketing department's asset ("digital gold, the crisis hedge") was supposed to be having its moment. Regular readers will remember that actual gold also fell that morning, and for the same reason: in a deleveraging, everything liquid becomes a source of funds. But gold had something bitcoin lacks: a price-insensitive buyer underneath (the central banks, 21 straight months of them), a bid that exists for reasons unrelated to charts. Bitcoin's version of that structural bid, it turned out, was something far more fragile, and its unwinding is the under-covered mechanical story of this entire bear market.

I mean the treasury companies. Recall the model, 2024-25's great financial innovation: a public company (Strategy the archetype, then dozens of imitators, a $150 billion boom at its peak) issues stock and convertible bonds, uses the proceeds to buy bitcoin, and watches its own shares trade at a premium to the coins it holds, a premium justified by the promise of issuing more stock to buy more coins. The stock's premium finances the next purchase; the purchase supports the coin; the coin supports the premium. A flywheel, powered by its own reflection. I hope three months of this newsletter (the Situational Awareness fund's four-times-levered conviction; private credit's inflow-dependent serenity; the index-fund plumbing that buys whatever's blessed) has installed the reflex that fires at the phrase "a flywheel powered by its own reflection."

In 2026 the reflection blinked. The premiums (the industry called the metric mNAV, market cap over coin value) collapsed toward and then below 1: the market began valuing the wrapper at less than its contents, which is a paradox that resolves either through buybacks or through selling the contents. By July the trade press was reporting the once unthinkable, plainly: treasury companies unwinding holdings, repaying debt, pivoting to whatever narrative was hiring (several, delightfully, to AI). Strategy itself, the never-sell standard-bearer, has sold bitcoin four separate times this year, with its stock below the value of its own coins. The structural bid of the bull market became the structural ask of the bear. There was never a mystery buyer of last resort under bitcoin. There was a leveraged corporate arbitrage wearing one's costume, and arbitrages run in both directions.

What bitcoin actually trades as

Assemble the year's evidence and the identity underneath becomes hard to unsee.

Falls when real yields rise. Falls when leverage anywhere gets called (July 8). Falls hardest when its own internal leverage (the treasury flywheel) unwinds. Rallies 6.6 percent on a presidential endorsement of a bill, on narrative fuel alone. Utterly indifferent, in either direction, to its founding story's milestones: the halvings, adoption metrics, the legal victories. Bitcoin in 2026 trades as what it has slowly become since the ETFs institutionalized it: the highest-beta expression of global liquidity conditions, a leveraged bet on how loose money feels, owned increasingly by the same funds, in the same portfolios, next to the same Nasdaq futures, sold by the same margin desks on the same bad days. The revolution got custodied. Once your ownership base is the system, you trade like the system, amplified.

And there's a subtler cost to the political victory, one I haven't seen priced anywhere, which is what the victory did to the story itself. Bitcoin's original pitch had a villain: the debasing, censoring, freezing state. The outlaw premium was real; an asset that resists confiscation is worth more in a world that confiscates (ask the central banks stacking gold after 2022; that logic built this year's only strong monetary-metal bid). But the American state has now, noisily, switched sides: it holds a reserve of the stuff, its First Family has commercial crypto ventures (which is precisely why the Clarity Act keeps stalling; Senate Democrats who like the bill dislike blessing the family business, the "Trump-sized problem" in the Capitol reporting, and Senator Warren's opposition writes itself), and its president pumps the price from a podium. What exactly is the resistance asset resisting? Gold solved this identity problem centuries ago by having no story at all, just physics and history; it can be the hedge for anyone against anyone. Bitcoin tied its monetary premium to an adversarial narrative, and the adversary defected, and a hedge against a government that endorses you is a product in search of its own necessity. The Clarity Act may pass in September, and it would be the final, fully clarified irony: the moment crypto becomes a legally ordinary asset class is the moment it must justify its price the way ordinary asset classes do. On cash flows (it has none), on utility (stablecoins, its one utility juggernaut, mostly enrich Treasury-bill holders), or on the greater fortitude of the next buyer.

None of this is a eulogy; the thing has died in the press four times my adult life and repaid every obituary with a new high. The honest framework is the one this series keeps returning to. An asset with no cash flows can only be priced, never valued, so the only analysis that matters is: who is the next marginal buyer, and what do they need to believe? The 2020-21 marginal buyer needed to believe in inflation hedging (broken: it fell with CPI at 9 and now falls with CPI at 3.4). The 2024-25 buyer needed to believe in institutional adoption and political blessing (delivered, fully, and now fully in the price, which is what "buy the rumor, sell the news" means when it takes three years). The next buyer needs something new to believe, and my candid read is that the only candidate on the horizon is the oldest one: loose money. Bitcoin's next real bull market starts when the global price of liquidity falls, whenever and for whatever reason that happens, and not one day before, no matter what passes the Senate.

Which yields the final markers of the summer, and fittingly they chain this essay to Tuesday's. Watch the Senate in September: if the Clarity Act passes and bitcoin cannot hold above $70,000 in the weeks after (its last positive catalyst, spent), the liquidity thesis is conclusively confirmed and the "regulatory overhang" excuse retires forever. Watch the treasury-company discounts: mNAV back above 1 across the sector would mean the reflexive machine is reloading, the one development that could power a story-rally without the Fed's help. And watch, above all, the same instrument I told you to watch on July 4 and again on Tuesday, because by now the whole summer has been one essay wearing seventeen hats: the long bond. Bitcoin was born in 2009, has lived its entire life inside one great era of cheap money, and has never once set a new high while the price of time was rising. The 30-year at 5.33 percent and bitcoin at $76,000 are the same fact, stated twice.

My closing line for the summer, then, is the one the whole run has been circling. Every asset in this newsletter's seventeen issues (oil, bonds, yen, gold, rockets, loans, coins) spent 2026 being repriced by the same force: the world's savers, for the first time in a generation, charging real money for time and trust. Everything that borrowed time cheaply (governments, carry trades, conviction funds, flywheels) is paying the new rate. Bitcoin asked to be treated as money. It is getting its wish. Money, this year, costs five and a third percent.
