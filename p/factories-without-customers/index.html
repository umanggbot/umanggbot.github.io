Factories Without Customers

Umang  ·  15 Jul 2026  ·  7 min read

This morning in Beijing, the National Bureau of Statistics released two numbers that cannot both describe a healthy economy.

This morning in Beijing, the National Bureau of Statistics released two numbers that cannot both describe a healthy economy.

The first: China's GDP grew 4.3 percent in the second quarter, the weakest reading since the end of 2022, when the country was still welding people into their apartments. It missed the consensus forecast, missed Beijing's own 4.5-to-5 percent target band, and marked a hard deceleration from 5 percent growth just one quarter earlier.

The second: exports in June were up 27 percent from a year ago.

Read those together. The overall economy is losing speed even though the part of it that sells to foreigners is growing at a pace normally associated with startups. Which means the part of it that sells to Chinese people is doing something much worse than 4.3 percent. And it is. Retail sales grew 1 percent in June, the third weak month in a row. Fixed-asset investment fell 5.7 percent in the first half. Property investment, the engine that once drove a quarter of Chinese economic activity, fell 18 percent, in year five of a decline that has now outlasted most marriages.

One country, two economies. The factories are sprinting. The households are hiding. Today's essay is about why that gap exists, why it is widening, and why the standard prescription (announce stimulus, wait, repeat) keeps failing to close it. Along the way I want to hand you the single most useful piece of arithmetic for reading any economy, because China right now is the clearest live demonstration of it on earth.

The arithmetic is this. Everything an economy produces has to be bought by someone. There are only four candidates: households (consumption), businesses and builders (investment), the government (spending), and foreigners (net exports). GDP is just the sum of the four. This sounds too simple to be useful, and it is the most useful sentence in macroeconomics, because it means that when one buyer disappears, the output they used to absorb has exactly three places to go, or it doesn't get produced, and somebody loses a job.

Run China through it. For thirty years, the second buyer did superhuman work: investment, above 40 percent of GDP, year after year, a share no large economy had ever sustained. Highways, ports, and above all apartments. Housing became the national savings account. Chinese households, with capital controls locking them out of foreign assets and a stock market that behaves like a casino with characteristics, put roughly two-thirds of their wealth into property. Developers borrowed against tomorrow's pre-sold apartments to build today's, local governments funded themselves by selling land to those developers, and everyone's balance sheet ended up resting on the same bet.

That bet broke in 2021, and the arithmetic has been grinding through the economy ever since. When property investment falls 18 percent, the first buyer of Chinese output (the construction complex) shrinks. Fine, says the textbook: households can pick up the slack. Except the same crash that idled the builders vaporized the households' net worth, because the apartments were the net worth. A family whose flat has lost a third of its value does not feel like shopping. It feels like saving, and that is what Chinese households are doing, relentlessly, in a way that no interest-rate cut has budged.

There's a name for this and it comes from Japan. The economist Richard Koo, watching Tokyo after its 1990 bust, called it a balance-sheet recession: the condition where households and firms, staring at damaged balance sheets, redirect income from spending to debt repair, and keep doing it whatever the central bank does, because you cannot rate-cut someone into feeling rich. Money gets cheaper; nobody wants it; the economy runs below capacity for years while everyone quietly heals. Japan needed the better part of two decades. China is in roughly year five, with a property overhang larger than Japan's was relative to its economy, and with one advantage Japan never had and one disadvantage Japan never faced.

The advantage is state control of the banks, which means no Lehman moment, no sudden cascade; the losses get socialized slowly, held on balance sheets at fictional marks, amortized across decades. The Chinese system does not do crashes. It does erosion.

The disadvantage is scale. Japan in 1995 was about 8 percent of the world economy and could export its way through its lost decades without anyone much minding. China is triple that share, and when it points its industrial machine outward, the world minds.

Which brings us to the 27 percent, because that number is the opposite of what it looks like. It looks like strength. It is the third buyer stepping in for the missing first two. When domestic consumption and investment cannot absorb what the factories produce, the output goes abroad, at whatever price it takes. Chinese electric vehicles, solar panels, batteries, steel, chemicals, machine tools: the export surge is the mirror image of the domestic collapse, the same overcapacity wearing its outbound uniform. Prices tell you it is a push, and China's producer prices have spent years in deflation. This is what it looks like when a $18 trillion economy tries to sell its surplus into a world that did not order it.

And 2026 handed that surplus a strange gift. In February, the US Supreme Court struck down the bulk of the American tariff wall, the IEEPA tariffs, on constitutional grounds. Whatever the domestic American politics of that decision, its trade meaning was immediate: the tax on Chinese goods entering their largest rich-world market fell sharply just as Chinese factories most needed somewhere to send output. Some of June's 27 percent is exactly that, cargo surging through a gate that a court unexpectedly propped open. The pushback will come from elsewhere, and it has already started: emerging economies from Brazil to India, watching their own manufacturers get undercut, have been raising trade barriers against Chinese goods all year. The next trade war will be less Washington versus Beijing, and more everyone versus overcapacity.

So why doesn't Beijing just fix it? The prescription is not mysterious, and every economist you ask recites the same one, including Cornell's Eswar Prasad, who summarized this week's data with the observation that China's economy "is becoming increasingly unbalanced": frontier-technology investment booming, consumption depressed. Give money to households. Build the pension and healthcare systems so families don't need to self-insure by hoarding savings. Accept slower headline growth with a healthier mix.

The answer to "why not" is that the imbalance is a policy, and behind it stands a worldview. The Chinese leadership has spent years signaling distrust of what it calls welfarism, the suspicion that direct transfers breed idleness, and a conviction that national strength lives in production, in factories and technology and export share, rather than in consumption. Money aimed at the economy keeps landing on the supply side: cheap credit for manufacturers, subsidies for priority industries, capacity, capacity, capacity. Each round makes the factories stronger and the glut worse. Stimulus that never touches the missing buyer cannot revive the missing buying.

Watch the Politburo's late-July economic meeting with that lens. The expectations run to targeted fiscal support, maybe consumption vouchers, accelerated local bond issuance. The tell will be the direction of the money. If it flows to households as spendable income, mark it as the turn, a genuine change of doctrine. If it flows, once again, to producers and projects, then the 27 percent gets larger, the 1 percent stays small, and the erosion continues on schedule. My money, with no joy in it, is on the second.

One more thing, the part I find quietly historic. For three decades, every Western business plan for China rested on a single sentence: a billion consumers are coming. The consumers arrived, briefly, in the 2010s, and then the property bust un-invited them. What exists now is the inversion of the dream: a country that produces like the future and consumes like the past, year five of a repair that outside estimates put closer to its beginning than its end. An urban unemployment rate of 5 percent and disposable income growing 5.2 percent sound respectable on paper, and are, and are also beside the point, because the issue was never income. It is the balance sheet, and balance sheets heal at the speed of fear.

Japan's version of this story ended, eventually, with corporate reform, a tourism boom, inflation imported at last, and a stock market that took 34 years to reclaim its 1989 high. Nikkei 38,915, December 1989; Nikkei 38,915 again, February 2024. Thirty-four years, round trip, for the patient.

The Shanghai Composite's all-time high is 6,124, set in October 2007. It trades today at roughly half that. If the balance-sheet playbook holds, somewhere out there is a February morning in the 2040s when Chinese state television gets to announce the recovery of a number first printed when the iPhone was four months old.

The factories will still be running. The question, then as now, is who buys.
