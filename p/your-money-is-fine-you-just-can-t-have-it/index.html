Your Money Is Fine, You Just Can't Have It

Umang  ·  18 Jul 2026  ·  8 min read

Say you put $500,000 into Blackstone's flagship private credit fund, BCRED, sometime in the boom.

Say you put $500,000 into Blackstone's flagship private credit fund, BCRED, sometime in the boom. It was an easy sale: a 9-plus percent yield, a portfolio of senior secured loans to solid American companies, statements that arrived every month showing a net asset value that basically never went down. Bonds without the mood swings. Your financial adviser probably owned it too.

This spring you decided you'd like some of the money back. Maybe the yield on plain Treasuries got competitive, maybe tuition came due, maybe the headlines about private credit started using words like "reckoning" and you decided you'd read enough. You submitted your redemption request like roughly ten percent of the fund's shareholders did in the second quarter, $4.4 billion of requests in all.

The fund sent back about 45 cents of each requested dollar, with a note explaining the rest would have to wait. On June 4, for the first time in its history, Blackstone had invoked the gate: a clause, sitting in the prospectus all along, capping total redemptions at 5 percent of the fund per quarter. Nothing was violated. Everything worked precisely as documented. Your money is fine, the marks say so, and you cannot have it.

Today's essay is about that clause, the trillion-and-a-half-dollar industry built on top of it, and the oldest trick in finance, which the clause was designed to survive. Because here is the sentence I want you to carry out of this piece: private credit did not invent a new asset class. It reinvented the bank, minus the deposit insurance, and this year the depositors started asking for their money.

The bank costume

Strip the branding and look at the machine. BCRED gathers money from hundreds of thousands of individuals who expect, within reason, to be able to get it back. It takes that money and makes loans to companies, loans that cannot be sold quickly or easily, at a markup. It layers on borrowed money of its own (about $36 billion of debt against $45 billion of investor capital, a leverage ratio of 0.8 times) to juice the yield. Collect deposits, hold illiquid loans, lever the book, pay depositors a spread. That is a bank. That has been the definition of a bank since Renaissance Italy.

And banks have one ancient failure mode. The loans come due in years; the depositors can ask today. If too many ask at once, the bank must sell loans fast, fast selling means bad prices, bad prices mean the last depositors out get less than whole, which is why everyone races to be first, which is the run. It is the most reliable catastrophe in financial history. Every structure that borrows short and lends long carries it like a gene.

Civilization's answer, after the 1930s burned half the American banking system down, was a bargain: banks got deposit insurance and a central bank willing to lend against good collateral in a panic, and in exchange they accepted examiners, capital rules, and limits on what they could hold. Runs mostly stopped. The bargain held for ninety years.

Private credit's founding insight was that you could have the bank without the bargain. No deposit insurance, no Fed backstop, no examiners second-guessing your marks. In exchange for giving all that up, you need some other answer to the run. Their answer is the gate. You cannot have a run if the depositors are contractually forbidden from running. Five percent per quarter, single file, no shoving.

I want to be fair to the design, because the design is clever. A gated fund cannot be forced into fire sales; it simply stops paying out. In a strict engineering sense the gate is a better run-stopper than deposit insurance, which merely discourages the stampede rather than banning it. The people who built these vehicles will tell you, correctly, that the gate is the product working as intended, disclosed on page whatever, initialed by every buyer.

All true. The trouble is what the existence of the gate does to the promise that sold the product. The pitch was bond-like income with bank-like stability. The reality, now visible, is bond-like income with hotel-California liquidity, and an exit that narrows at exactly the moment you want it. Liquidity is like oxygen in this specific way: its market price is approximately zero right up until the moment it is approximately infinite.

The marks

Now the part of the story I find most interesting, which is the question of what the money you cannot withdraw is actually worth.

BCRED reports a net asset value per share. That figure was $24.79 in December, $24.19 in March, $23.94 at the end of May: a stately, dignified 3.4 percent decline for the year. On the same statements, the portfolio's ingredients were moving less politely. Loans on non-accrual (the industry's term for borrowers who have stopped paying) jumped from 0.6 percent of the book at cost in the first quarter to 2.4 percent in the second. Quadrupled, in ninety days. A $1.1 billion loan to the software company Medallia is marked at 60 cents on the dollar. A position in Affordable Care fell to 70. The July distribution was cut to 18 cents a share, the second cut in nine months; it was 22 cents a year ago.

Hold those two pictures side by side. The headline NAV glides down a few percent a year, the way an administrator would draw it. The underlying book is producing quadrupling non-accruals and sixty-cent marks. Both come from the same building. The difference between the pictures is that nothing in the portfolio trades, so the value of everything is, within professional bounds, an opinion. The industry's critics have a name for the serenity this produces; the investor Cliff Asness has spent years calling it volatility laundering. The loans are just as risky as public credit, often riskier. What's been removed is the reporting of the risk.

If you want to know what happens when an actual market price intrudes on this arrangement, the industry provided a demonstration in the first quarter, and it is the single most clarifying number in this entire essay. Investors in Blue Owl's OTIC, a smaller non-traded vehicle, requested redemption of 40.7 percent of the fund. What the sellers who found exits received, in a secondary transaction, was 12 cents on the requested dollar of face. The statement says par-ish. The exit said twelve cents. Between a mark and a market there is sometimes a gap, and the gap is where careers, retirements, and lawsuits live.

To be scrupulously clear: BCRED is not OTIC. Blackstone's fund is enormous, 95 percent senior secured, has returned 9.8 percent annually since inception, and its 2.2 times interest coverage says the average borrower still earns its payments twice over. The system's defenders point to all of this, accurately. The reply, which 2026 keeps writing in larger font, is that averages are how credit trouble hides. Non-accruals quadrupling is not an average misbehaving. It is the tail arriving.

Where the run went

The industry-wide numbers say the tail has company. Across the non-traded funds, investors asked for $13.9 billion back in the first quarter; $4.6 billion of it was gated. New fundraising, the inflow side that made every prior exit painless, fell 74 percent in April from a year earlier. That last number deserves a beat of silence, because these vehicles were engineered around a current: money in the front, money out the back, the gate never needed as long as the front door stayed busy. The front door has gone quiet. What remains is a $79 billion portfolio, a queue at the back, and arithmetic.

Two threads make this bigger than one industry's awkward year.

First: who ends up owning these loans. The same managers running the retail funds also run private credit for insurance companies, which have loaded annuity portfolios (the thing your grandmother's retirement check comes out of) with exactly this paper, because the accounting treats unquoted assets gently. And the banks that were supposedly disrupted by private credit turn out to be its lenders: a large share of fund leverage, including a chunk of that $36 billion, is bank credit lines. The risk left the regulated system through the front door and re-entered through the loading dock, wearing a costume. Regulators spent fifteen years congratulating themselves that the banks were safe. The question of 2026 is what the banks are safe from, exactly, if they financed the thing that replaced them.

Second: why the borrowers are wobbling now. Read the names going bad and a pattern shows up. Medallia: enterprise software. A striking share of the loans souring across these books were 2021-vintage lending to software and services companies, the kind with recurring revenue that private credit considered the safest collateral on earth. Recurring, it turns out, means recurring until your customer replaces you, and 2026's corporate software budgets are being rewritten by AI at a pace nobody's covenant models contemplated. The safest collateral of the last cycle is the disruption surface of this one. I'll have much more to say about the AI unwind in a few weeks, but note the connection now: the same force deflating the Nasdaq's dreams is quietly defaulting the loans inside America's retirement products.

So, the markers I'd put down. Blackstone's own precedent, the BREIT property-fund gate of November 2022, took 18 months to lift, and that was with the Nasdaq roaring and rate cuts hoped for. BCRED's gate, in my judgment, outlasts BREIT's unless the default tide turns within two quarters, and the number that tells you is non-accruals in the Q3 report this autumn: under 2 percent, the machine restabilizes; anywhere near 4, the queue at the back door doubles and the distribution gets cut a third time. And watch for the year's quietest important trade: publicly traded BDCs already price at deep discounts to stated NAV, which means the stock market has already voted on what the marks are worth. The gate can stop the depositors. It cannot stop the repricing.

None of this required anyone to lie. That's what I find most instructive about it. Every clause was disclosed, every mark defensible, every yield real while it lasted. The industry simply rebuilt the oldest machine in finance, told everyone it was something new, and trusted that the tide would never ask everyone the same question on the same day. Banking's entire history is the story of that question eventually getting asked. This time the answer, printed politely at the bottom of half a million account statements, is: five percent per quarter, and thank you for your patience.
