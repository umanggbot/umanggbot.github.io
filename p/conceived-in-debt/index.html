Conceived in Debt

Umang  ·  4 Jul 2026  ·  9 min read

On the evening of June 20, 1790, Thomas Jefferson hosted a small dinner at his rented house on Maiden Lane in New York. Two guests: Alexander Hamilton and James Madison. Hamilton arrived desperate. His plan for the federal government to assume the states' unpaid war debts, about $25 million, the load-bearing wall of his entire financial architecture, was dying in Congress. Madison was the one killing it.

By dessert they had a deal. Madison would stop whipping votes against assumption. In exchange, the new national capital would go south, to a swamp on the Potomac. The United States got its debt plan and Virginia got Washington, D.C., and if you ever want to explain American politics to a foreigner, you can do worse than: we put the capital where it is as part of a debt restructuring.

Today the republic turns 250. The debt Hamilton consolidated came to roughly $75 million. The debt outstanding this morning is a touch under $40 trillion. Call it a 530,000-fold increase, against a population that grew 85-fold. And the fact I want to dwell on is stranger than either number: for the entire 250 years, someone respectable has been announcing that the debt is about to ruin us, the ruin has never arrived, and yet the people saying it this year might finally have a point. Not because the number got big. The number was always big. Because of who has quietly stopped showing up to lend.

Since it is a birthday, let's open the gifts. Who actually holds the IOUs of the United States?

Start with the strangest chunk: about $8 trillion, a fifth of the gross figure, the government owes to itself. Social Security collects payroll taxes, doesn't need all of them yet, and parks the surplus in Treasury securities, because the law says it must. One drawer of the federal dresser holding a note from another drawer. Economists argue about whether this even counts as debt. My favorite way to think about it: a family that keeps its vacation savings in a jar, borrows from the jar for groceries, and leaves a note. The note is real. The family's total wealth is unchanged. But the vacation still has to be paid for, and the note-writer and the jar-owner are, awkwardly, the same person at different ages. That $8 trillion is a promise from present taxpayers to future retirees with extra paperwork.

Now the $21 trillion or so held domestically outside the family jar. The Federal Reserve holds about $4.5 trillion, the residue of crisis-era bond buying that it has been letting shrink for years. Mutual funds hold about $5.2 trillion, which very much includes you, if you have a 401(k) with a target-date fund: some slice of your retirement is a loan to the Treasury, whether or not you've ever thought of yourself as a bond investor. Commercial banks hold about $2.1 trillion. Pension funds another $1.1 trillion. State and local governments, $1.6 trillion. Insurers, corporations, households directly, a few trillion more. The largest creditor of the United States, by an enormous margin, is Americans.

That is the origin of the old comfort, "we owe it to ourselves." Which is about one-third true. The other roughly 32 percent of the marketable stuff is held abroad, and it is inside that 32 percent that the actual 2026 story lives.

Japan is the largest foreign creditor, at about $1.2 trillion. Second place is the United Kingdom at $889 billion, though "the United Kingdom" here mostly means the City of London, hedge funds and asset managers custodying bonds for clients from everywhere, finance's equivalent of a mail-forwarding address. And China, which for most of my adult life was the boogeyman of every debt polemic, the creditor who supposedly had America by the throat: $683 billion. Third place, and shrinking.

Read that again, because the polemics haven't updated. China held around $1.3 trillion at the peak, in 2013. It has since let nearly half of that roll off, methodically, year after year, without drama, replaced at the margin by gold in the People's Bank vaults. The creditor everyone feared didn't dump the bonds. It just stopped renewing them, the way you quit a gym: no announcement, you just don't go back.

Which brings us to the machine itself, because "the debt" is mostly misunderstood as a pile when it is actually a conveyor belt.

The United States never repays its debt. This scandalizes people, and it shouldn't; no large country repays its debt, any more than a bank "repays" its depositors all at once. What the Treasury does is roll it. Bonds mature constantly, every week, and the Treasury pays them off by selling new bonds to whoever shows up at that week's auction. The average maturity of the whole book is about six years. Hamilton's original insight, the one he traded a capital city for, was exactly this: a sovereign debt is never really a sum owed, it is a relationship with a market, and the only thing that matters is that the market keeps answering the phone.

So the real exposure is never the principal. Nobody will ever knock on the door asking for the $40 trillion. The exposure is the rate at which the conveyor belt gets refinanced. Old debt issued in the 2010s and early 2020s carries coupons of 1 and 2 percent. Every week, a slab of it matures and gets reissued at today's rates, which are 4-something on the short end and pushing 5 on the long end. The blended interest rate on the whole book grinds upward mechanically, a mortgage that refloats itself in slow motion, and there is nothing any Congress can do about the part that has already happened.

Here is where the numbers stop being trivia. Net interest is running at about $1.1 trillion a year. That is 19 cents of every dollar of federal revenue, before the government buys a single aircraft carrier, processes a single Social Security check, or pays a single park ranger. It exceeds the defense budget. In March the total crossed $39 trillion; the pace of accumulation works out to about $90,000 per second, which means that in the twelve or so minutes it takes to read this essay, the republic will have borrowed roughly another $65 million, about what Hamilton assumed from all thirteen states combined, adjusted for nothing, because adjustment would ruin a perfectly good sentence.

And this year's deficit will run past $2 trillion in peacetime. Except, of course, it is not exactly peacetime. There was a war this spring; wars cost money and suppress nobody's spending. The Supreme Court in February struck down the tariff program that was supposed to bring in hundreds of billions, and the Treasury now owes importers refunds instead. Revenue lighter than promised, spending heavier than planned, on a book that reprices itself upward every week. That is the fiscal picture on the country's 250th birthday.

Now, the case for calm, because there is one, and it has won the argument for 250 straight years. The United States borrows in a currency it prints. It cannot be forced into the kind of default that ruined Argentina; the machine can always technically pay. American debt is the world's savings mattress: when anything anywhere goes wrong, money runs toward Treasuries, a reflex demonstrated as recently as March, when a literal war in the Persian Gulf sent buyers into the very bonds funding the belligerent. The republic has carried debt loads that looked apocalyptic before: 106 percent of GDP in 1946, worked off over three decades through growth and inflation without a single missed coupon. Anyone who bet on American fiscal collapse at any point since Maiden Lane has lost, and most of them lost embarrassingly.

The bears' problem was never the arithmetic. It was the tense. The debt is unsustainable, they say, and the market hears: not today, then, and goes back to lunch.

But something has changed, and the number's size is the wrong place to look for it. Look instead at the composition of the people at the auctions.

For decades the marginal buyer of Treasuries was, in one costume or another, someone who had to buy. Foreign central banks recycling trade surpluses, because where else do you put a trillion dollars. The Fed, buying by the trillion to fight crises. Pension funds matching liabilities under rules that treated Treasuries as the risk-free default. Price-insensitive, all of them. They didn't ask what the yield was. They asked how much was available.

That cast is retiring. China: down $600 billion from peak and still drifting away. The Fed: shrinking its holdings on schedule, a seller in net terms. The trade surpluses that needed recycling: smaller, and increasingly recycled into gold instead, which is a sentence that would have sounded medieval in 2015 and is now simply a fact of reserve management. Who replaced them? Mutual funds, hedge funds, households, the London custody chain. Investors, in other words. People who compare yields, read Congressional Budget Office reports, and can leave.

The debt didn't change. The lender did. The mortgage analogy from earlier needs its final turn: the house never mattered, and even the size of the mortgage never quite mattered, so long as the bank was your uncle. The bank is no longer your uncle. It is a fund manager in London with a Bloomberg terminal and no particular feelings about the Fourth of July.

None of this produces a crisis on a schedule, and I am not predicting one; the honest record of debt-doom predictions is 0 for 250, and humility is warranted. What a buyer rotation produces instead is a price. Investors who can leave don't storm out; they ask for more yield, a quarter point here, a half point there, a weak auction that "tails" on a Tuesday afternoon nobody remembers. The bill for 250 years of it-always-works arrives as a rate. There is never a bang.

So here is the marker I'll put down on the republic's birthday. Ignore the debt-clock billboards; the number will cross $40 trillion soon and the billboard will not tell you anything the $39 trillion didn't. Watch the 30-year bond instead. The 30-year is the pure expression of the question who wants to lend America money for a generation, at what price, with no obligation to say yes. If the buyer rotation I've described is real, it will show up there first, as a yield that grinds toward and then through 5 percent and stays there even on the days when stocks wobble and money is supposed to hide in bonds. That is the tell. The short end belongs to the Fed. The long end belongs to the willing.

Two hundred fifty years ago the country's first Treasury Secretary bet the capital city on a simple proposition: that the United States, if it paid reliably, would never run out of willing lenders. He was right for longer than any financial forecast in recorded history. He also wrote, in a line his fans quote less often, that a national debt would be a blessing only "if it is not excessive."

He never defined excessive. He didn't have to. The auctions define it, one Tuesday at a time.
