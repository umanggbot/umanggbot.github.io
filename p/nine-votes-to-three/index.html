Nine Votes to Three

Umang  ·  29 Jul 2026  ·  8 min read

The Federal Reserve did nothing today, and it took a 9-3 vote to do it.

The Federal Reserve did nothing today, and it took a 9-3 vote to do it.

The federal funds rate stays at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, where it has sat for five consecutive meetings. That's the non-news. The news is the arithmetic of the room: three members of the committee (Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Lorie Logan of Dallas, and Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis) formally dissented because they wanted to raise rates, and the futures market, by tonight's close, is pricing two quarter-point hikes before New Year's, one in September, one in December.

Read that again if it went by fast. Hikes. In the third year after the inflation emergency was declared over, with a new chair handpicked by a president who campaigned on cheap money, the world's most important central bank spent today arguing about whether to make money more expensive, and the argument was close. The last time three FOMC members dissented in the same direction at one meeting, hawkishly, you have to go back decades. Committees this size are engineered for consensus. Consensus cracked today, in public, and I want to walk through why, because the why is a better education in monetary policy than any textbook chapter on it.

The pedal

Start with the question a smart twelve-year-old would ask: inflation has been running in the mid-3s against a 2 percent target (3.8 percent on the spring CPI prints, with producer prices at 6), and it has been above target for five years. Why is the Fed sitting still at all? Isn't sitting still, when you're missing your target, just missing it on purpose?

The answer requires the one concept that explains nearly all central-bank behavior: the brake pedal is measured from inflation, not from zero.

An interest rate of 3.625 percent (the midpoint of the current range) sounds restrictive the way any number sounds after fifteen years of zero. But borrowers and savers don't experience the number; they experience the number minus inflation. If prices rise around 3.5 percent a year and cash earns 3.625, the real return on money is a rounding error above zero. That is the actual pressure on the brake: roughly nothing. The Fed is driving a car whose speedometer reads three-and-a-half in a 2.0 zone, and its foot is resting on the brake with approximately the force of a house cat.

How did the foot get so light? That's the part today's dissenters cannot say aloud, so I will. Through 2025, the Fed cut rates repeatedly, from the 5-percent-plus plateau of the inflation fight down to today's 3.5-to-3.75, on a forecast that inflation was gliding to 2 and the labor market needed insurance. The glide never finished. Inflation stalled in the mid-3s, and then 2026 attacked the forecast from three directions at once: a war that put a floor under oil, gasoline back near $4, and, per the spring's data, producer prices running at 6. The cuts were premature, is the plain reading, delivered on a forecast that didn't survive contact with the year. Central banks are run by humans; humans hate reversing; reversing after cutting is a confession with a press conference attached.

There's a name for the pattern everyone in that room is privately terrified of, and it comes from the 1970s: stop-go. The Fed of Arthur Burns would tighten against inflation, see unemployment twitch, lose its nerve, ease, watch inflation come back stronger, and repeat, each cycle teaching the public that the Fed's pain tolerance was low, which taught wage-setters and price-setters to disbelieve the Fed entirely, which is how you get to 1979, when it took Paul Volcker, 20 percent interest rates, and the worst recession since the war to buy the credibility back. Every modern central banker carries that story like a vaccination scar. Today's statement, with its careful acknowledgment that inflation "remains elevated," is the sound of an institution trying very hard not to be the Burns Fed, while positioned exactly where the Burns Fed kept standing.

The dissenters' case, then, in one sentence: the real rate is nearly zero, inflation has been above target for half a decade, the war has re-armed the price shocks, and every month of patience spends credibility the institution may shortly need. And there is a human detail in the trio worth savoring. Neel Kashkari spent 2017 through 2019 as the FOMC's loudest dove, dissenting against rate hikes, three of them, arguing the Fed was strangling a recovery on phantom inflation fears. Same man, opposite dissent, nine years later. You can read that as inconsistency. I read it as one of the few unambiguous data points about the situation itself: when the committee's most famous dove starts voting for hikes, the argument that this is all hawkish reflex has a Kashkari-shaped hole in it.

The chair

Which brings us to the man running his third meeting, and the strangest political geometry in Washington.

Kevin Warsh took the chair in late May, the first Fed chief in decades who is neither an economics PhD nor a career academic: a lawyer by training, Morgan Stanley by formation, a Fed governor through the 2008 crisis, and for the past decade the most prominent conservative critic of the institution he now leads, author of a hundred op-eds arguing the Fed had grown too big, too chatty, too fond of financing deficits, too certain of models that kept being wrong. The president who appointed him wants rate cuts, has said so at volumes ranging from post to podium, and inherited from his own first term the habit of treating the funds rate as a scoreboard. The chair that president chose now presides over a committee whose internal pressure is pointed at hikes.

Something has to give, and today you could watch Warsh's answer take shape: what gives is the talking.

Under his predecessors, the Fed practiced "forward guidance," the art of pre-announcing the path of rates so markets could price it smoothly. Dot plots, "patient," "transitory," calendars of intent. Warsh has begun dismantling the apparatus, saying less about the future, letting meetings be live, letting the data surprise. Traders hate it; the whole two-hikes-priced-by-December structure is the market's attempt to guess a path the chair now refuses to sketch. My own view, for what it's worth, is that the retreat from guidance is the most defensible thing the new regime has done. Guidance was born as an emergency tool at the zero bound and metastasized into a subsidy: a standing promise that markets would never be surprised, which taught markets to run maximum leverage against minimum uncertainty, which made each eventual surprise systemic. A central bank that reserves the right to surprise you is a central bank you position for honestly, with smaller bets. The howling from trading desks is the sound of a subsidy being repriced. It is also, not incidentally, the sound of a chair discovering that silence is the only tool that offends the White House and the bond market equally.

Because note what Warsh did not do today, in either direction. No hike, though his career's writings argue for one. No cut, though his patron demands it. A 9-3 hold, a statement acknowledging "elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East," and a refusal to promise anything about September. You can call it paralysis. You can also call it the only position from which the institution's independence survives the year, a chair proving to a hostile market and a hungrier White House that the committee, and only the committee, moves the rate. The most consequential thing about today may simply be that the president's chairman declined to deliver the president's rate cut, at a meeting where the live alternative was a hike.

One more mechanical note, the kind that never makes the recap videos. A war is the worst possible weather for a central bank because it breaks the tool. Oil at $78 because of missiles is inflation the funds rate cannot touch; the Fed can't print barrels, and hiking against a supply shock taxes the economy twice. The textbook says look through it. The 1970s say looking through it is how the anchor slips. The committee's split today is, at bottom, a disagreement about which of those two sentences carries the weight right now, and no PhD, and no lack of one, settles it. The models simply do not know. That, and no personality clash, is why the vote cracked.

So, markers, and this time the calendar is kind enough to be specific. Two inflation reports land before the committee reconvenes September 15-16. If they keep printing in the mid-3s, I say the dissenters win and the hike happens, the first in over three years, and the political explosion (a Trump-appointed chair raising rates into a midterm year) will be the finance story of the autumn. If inflation finally cracks lower, the hold holds, the doves exhale, and the two-hikes pricing dies quietly. Either way, watch one instrument for the verdict on all of it: the 30-year Treasury, which cares nothing for committee politics and prices only one thing, whether anyone still believes the long-run inflation promise. It has been drifting up all month, through today's decision, which is the bond market's way of clearing its throat.

Ninety-nine years ago this institution was invented so that one man's judgment would never again be the financial system's last line of defense. Today its nineteen judgments split three ways under one roof, which is either the system failing or the system working, and I keep coming back to the suspicion that it's both: the committee was built to average away conviction, and 2026 keeps sending it problems that refuse to be averaged.

Next meeting, someone has to be wrong out loud. September 16. Mark it.
