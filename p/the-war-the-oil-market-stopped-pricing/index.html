The War the Oil Market Stopped Pricing

Umang  ·  1 Jul 2026  ·  10 min read

On February 27 of this year, a barrel of Brent crude cost about $72.

On February 27 of this year, a barrel of Brent crude cost about $72. Last Thursday, June 25, it cost $72.68.

Between those two prices there was a war. There is still a war, or at least a 60-day pause with a war attached to both ends of it. Iranian missiles, American carrier groups, a mined Strait of Hormuz, gasoline at $3.99 a gallon, diesel at $5.40, emergency cabinet meetings on four continents. Brent went through $100 in the second week of March and at the worst printed within a dollar of $120. And then, over three months, the entire thing was unwound, dollar by dollar, until the price arrived back where it started, like a tide going out and taking the evidence with it.

If you fell asleep on February 27 and woke up last week and looked only at the oil price, you would conclude that nothing had happened.

Something happened. So the interesting question, the one I want to spend the next few thousand words on, is what exactly the oil market thinks a war is. Because whatever it thinks, it is plainly something different from what the rest of us think, and the difference is one of the more useful things you can understand about how prices work.

What the market saw

Quick reconstruction, for anyone who spent the spring under professional obligations to look away.

The war began on February 28. Within days, the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile-wide channel that roughly a fifth of the world's oil transits, was under a de facto closure. Not an announced closure with a press release. Mines, drones, and insurance rates did the closing. Tankers idled. Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE shut in production, because pumping oil you cannot ship is a hobby, and these are commercial operations.

The price did what you'd expect. Brent had started the year around $61. It ended February at $72 as the buildup got loud. Then March: through $100, a spike to nearly $120, retail gasoline in America at its highest real price in over two years by month-end. The US Energy Information Administration later noted, with the placid tone of an agency that files its panic under appendices, that strong domestic inventories and planned releases from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve helped limit the damage on the American side.

One detail from that stretch tells you more than the headline price, and I keep returning to it. At the start of the year, Brent (the global, seaborne benchmark) traded about $4 above WTI (the American, landlocked one). By March 31 the gap was $25. The average spread for March was $11, the widest in over five years.

Stop on that for a second. Crude oil is crude oil. A refinery does not check a barrel's passport for sentimental reasons; it checks because location is the product. In March, the same fungible molecule was worth $25 more if it happened to be floating near Rotterdam than if it was sitting in a tank in Cushing, Oklahoma. The war exposed something more useful than a higher oil price: the fact that there is no such thing. There are prices of oil in places, and a chokepoint is a machine for prying those prices apart. America, pumping 13-plus million barrels a day at home, an ocean away from the mines, was suddenly an island, and island economics are their own economics.

The other tell was the shape of the futures curve, which most news coverage skips because curves don't photograph well. During the worst of it, the front of the curve was screaming while the back of the curve yawned. Oil for delivery next month went vertical; oil for delivery in 2028 barely moved. Depending on which contract month you squinted at, "the oil price" at the end of March was anywhere from $104 to $118. That gap between the front and the back is the market saying something specific: we have a problem today, and we do not believe we will have this problem for long.

Hold that thought, because the market turned out to mean it.

The bookmaker

The mental model I'd offer comes with a warning: I'm going to push it until it breaks, because the place where it breaks is the actual point of this essay.

The oil futures market is a bookmaker, and it is pricing a fight.

A bookmaker does not set odds by deciding who deserves to win. He does not price outrage, or history, or whose cause is just. He prices flows. Money on one side, money on the other, and the line moves until the flows balance. When commentators say a market is "complacent" about a war, they are usually making a category error. The market is not offering an opinion about the war. It is offering a price at which barrels change hands, and barrels, unlike opinions, can be counted.

So price a war the way the bookmaker would. A war matters to the price of oil through exactly one channel: barrels that would have reached buyers, and don't. Everything else (the rhetoric, the strikes on things that are not oil infrastructure, the terrifying maps on television) is noise, in the strict sense that it does not subtract barrels. The March spike was the market's estimate of subtracted barrels: probability of disruption, times size, times duration. Something like an insurance premium, recalculated every second.

And then, from late March onward, each component of that premium got marked down in public.

Demand did some of the work. Gasoline at $3.99 is its own quiet policy intervention. People drive less, airlines trim schedules, factories in price-sensitive countries slow down. Nobody votes on it, it just happens, a few hundred thousand barrels a day of consumption evaporating because the price told them to.

Inventories did more of the work. The world, it turns out, keeps a pantry. Commercial tanks, strategic reserves, oil sitting in ships. When the strait shut, the world did not stop eating; it ate from the pantry. The EIA credited American inventories and SPR plans with capping the domestic spike, and every other consuming nation was doing a version of the same thing.

Then, decisively, the barrels came back. By late June, tanker traffic through Hormuz was approaching pre-war volumes, with at least 20 million barrels exiting the strait in a single 24-hour window. Oman opened temporary shipping corridors. The Gulf producers that had shut in production, for want of any way to ship it, began reversing course, and the market started sniffing a further twist: Iran itself, under temporary sanctions relief attached to the talks, positioned to sell more oil into the recovery, its own war financed in part by the reopening it had been threatening.

And there was, of course, the ceasefire itself. On June 17 the parties signed a 14-point memorandum, brokered with Pakistani mediation, that set a 60-day negotiating window; on June 22, in Switzerland, negotiators agreed a roadmap toward a final deal. Mines still need clearing, and full normality in the strait is reckoned in weeks. Iran spent last Thursday warning against "unauthorized" crossings, which is the kind of thing you say when you want everyone to remember you could change your mind.

Add it up the way the bookmaker does. Fewer barrels lost than feared, ample barrels stored, barrels returning faster than expected, and a signed piece of paper suggesting the whole thing winds down. The line moves. $119 becomes $95 becomes $80 becomes, by June 25, $72.68: the pre-war price, exactly. The odds have gone back to the pre-fight line. The market has priced the war at zero.

There is even a small flourish in the curve to confirm it. August Brent closed that day at $72.68 while September closed at $73.59. The front month cheaper than the next one, a mild contango, is the market's way of saying near-term supply is ample. Four months ago the curve was inverted and shrieking. Now it slouches. Markets do not do relief rallies in oil; they do boredom, and boredom, in this market, is a price signal with real information in it.

Where the pantry analogy breaks

I promised the model would break. Here is where.

A bookmaker settles his book when the fight ends. The oil market's fight has not ended. It has a tarp over it, and the tarp has a date printed on it. The 60-day clock in the June 17 memorandum runs out in mid-August. As of today, that is about 47 days away. The market is treating the deadline as scenery. Deadlines are scenery, right up until they are cliffs.

And the pantry problem is worse than the price implies, which is the part I find under-discussed to the point of negligence. The world got through March, April, and May by eating stored barrels: commercial inventories drawn, strategic reserves committed, OPEC's shut-in capacity absorbing the shock in both directions. Those buffers are how a fifth of the world's seaborne oil supply got interrupted and the whole episode ended up costing drivers a bad spring rather than a global recession.

But a pantry, once eaten, is not a pantry. It's shelving.

Refilling takes months at best. Strategic reserves are refilled at political speed, which is slower than any other speed known to engineering. Shut-in production comes back, but Gulf spare capacity, the famous cushion that market coverage invokes like a superstition, has already been partly converted from cushion into supply. Run the same shock again in the autumn, against drawn inventories and thinner spare capacity, and the same barrels-times-duration arithmetic produces a very different price path. The first spike was absorbed by buffers built over years. The second one would land on the shelving.

Which brings us to the strangest thing about $72.68. It is the same price as February 27, but it is describing a different world. February's $72 had a full pantry behind it and no war. June's $72.68 has a depleted pantry, a mined strait being swept, a signed memorandum with 47 days on the clock, and a regional order held together by negotiators in Switzerland. The number is identical. The situation the number claims to summarize is not even similar. If you want a one-sentence education in the limits of price as information, there it is: price is a summary statistic, and like all summary statistics it achieves its tidiness by throwing information away.

The people closest to the physical trade are not acting bored, incidentally. Somewhere in the strait this week, crews are sweeping for mines so that a tanker can load on schedule, and the tanker is loading on schedule because a memorandum says the missiles will stay in their tubes for another few weeks. Every quoted price you see rests on an assumption that thousands of such people, none of whom you will ever hear named, keep succeeding at their jobs. The screen shows $72.68. The screen is very confident. The minesweepers are still out there, which tells you what the people who handle the actual barrels think confidence is worth.

What I'd actually take from this

Two things, and then a marker you can hold me to.

The first is a reading habit. When a geopolitical shock hits a commodity, ignore the level and read the spreads: the gap between benchmarks (Brent minus WTI told you March's true story better than either price alone), and the gap between the front and back of the curve. The level tells you that people are scared. The spreads tell you where the barrels aren't, and how long the market expects the problem to last. All the actual information density lives in the differences.

The second is about what "the market has priced it in" means. Nobody concluded the war is over. The current flow of barrels, adjusted by the current stock of buffers, simply stopped justifying a premium. That is a statement about logistics, with a time horizon of weeks, and it carries no warranty beyond that horizon. Treating a logistics verdict as a geopolitical forecast is how people got surprised in February, and I suspect it is how some people will get surprised again.

So, the marker. The strip is pricing peace as the base case: not as a hope, as a base case, at full weight, with the pantry thin. If the mid-August deadline passes into a real agreement, oil in the 60s is defensible and this essay becomes a curiosity. If the talks fail with the buffers where they are, the next spike starts from a weaker floor than February's and travels further. I don't know which it will be, and neither does the strip; the difference between us is that the strip is obligated to pick a number anyway, and it has picked one that assumes the tarp holds.

$72.68 is the market's way of saying the fire is out. What the market means is that the fire is under a tarp, the extinguishers are half-empty, and everyone has agreed to check back in August.

Certainty is the one commodity the Gulf has never exported. It is currently trading at par.
