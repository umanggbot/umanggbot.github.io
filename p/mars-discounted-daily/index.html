Mars, Discounted Daily

Umang  ·  11 Jul 2026  ·  9 min read

In December, SpaceX ran an insider share sale that valued the company at $800 billion.

In December, SpaceX ran an insider share sale that valued the company at $800 billion. Six months later, on June 11, it sold stock to the public at $135 a share, implying roughly $1.5 trillion. By the close of the first trading day, June 12, the ticker SPCX stood at $161, a 19 percent pop, and the company that had been worth $800 billion at Christmas was worth about $1.79 trillion by the following summer solstice.

One month on, the pop is gone. The stock has spent this week drifting back down through the $140s, pushed by the same two forces squeezing everything else with an AI story attached (a tech selloff that started in June, and a war that un-paused on Tuesday), and it is now knocking on its own IPO price from above.

So in seven months, the market's answer to the question "what is SpaceX worth" has been: $800 billion, then $1.5 trillion, then $1.79 trillion, then maybe $1.5 trillion again, and the company itself did approximately nothing in that time except continue being SpaceX. Launches launched. Starlink signed up subscribers. No new physics. The rockets are the same rockets; the number under them moved by a trillion dollars, twice, in both directions.

If that strikes you as strange, good. That strangeness is the most educational thing in markets right now, and today I want to use it to explain the single most useful idea in finance, which is also the most quietly radical: the difference between what something trades for and what it is worth. The best living guide to that difference, NYU's Aswath Damodaran, published a full valuation of SpaceX in April, before the IPO, and his numbers give us something almost unheard of in a mania: a benchmark that was written down in advance.

The machine that discounts

First, the idea, built from a lemonade stand, because I promised myself this newsletter would always be willing to go all the way down to the studs.

Your kid runs a lemonade stand. It reliably clears $100 every summer. What's the stand worth? Well: $100 next summer, but a dollar promised in a year is worth a bit less than a dollar in your hand (you could have banked today's dollar and earned interest, and also, the kid might quit). So you shave next summer's $100 down to, say, $93. Summer after that, shave twice. Add up every future summer, each one shaved harder than the last, and the pile of shaved dollars is what the stand is worth today. That's a discounted cash flow. That's the whole thing. Every valuation model on Wall Street is that arithmetic wearing progressively more expensive clothing.

Two dials control everything. How big the future cash flows get (the story dial), and how hard you shave them (the doubt dial). All financial screaming, everywhere, forever, is people disagreeing about one dial or the other.

Now watch what happens when the lemonade stand is a rocket company.

Damodaran's April valuation did exactly what the lemonade math demands, just with better ingredients. He broke SpaceX into its actual businesses and gave each one a story with numbers attached. The launch business, which currently moves more than 80 percent of everything humanity sends to orbit: he projected it to about $70 billion of revenue by 2036 with 40 percent operating margins. Starlink, the satellite broadband arm and already the bulk of current revenue: $100 billion by 2036 at 60 percent margins. The xAI piece, folded into the company this year: $80 billion at 50 percent. Then a bucket for the dreams with unclear invoices (space tourism, whatever Mars is commercially) at another $50 billion. Discount the whole thing at an 8 percent doubt dial and you get a value of about $1.22 trillion. He ran the model 10,000 times with the dials jittered randomly; the median came out at $1.29 trillion.

Pause on what those inputs concede. They concede near-monopoly in launch, forever-dominance in satellite internet, a successful AI business, and margins that almost no industrial company in history sustains, and the answer still comes out at $1.22 trillion. Against that: the IPO asked $1.5 trillion, and day-one buyers paid $1.79 trillion.

For scale, SpaceX's actual 2025 financials were about $15.6 billion of revenue and roughly $8 billion of EBITDA. At the day-one close, the market was paying around 81 times revenue and 156 times EBITDA. One hundred fifty-six years of current cash earnings, for a company whose main product operates on top of controlled explosions. The optimists' answer is that current earnings are irrelevant because the future is enormous, and the honest response is: correct, that is exactly the bet, now say it with the number attached. At $1.79 trillion you were not buying SpaceX the going concern. You were buying Damodaran's most generous scenario, plus a tip.

The two prices

Which returns us to the trillion-dollar wobble, because here's the naive question I promised: how does the same company get marked at $800 billion in December and $1.79 trillion in June? Nothing about the rockets changed. What changed?

The buyer changed.

December's $800 billion was a price set by insiders and funds in a private sale: patient money, negotiated access, spreadsheets out. June's $1.79 trillion was set at the margin by whoever wanted 100 shares badly enough at 9:31 a.m., which included, by all accounts, an enormous retail wave for whom SPCX was less a security than a souvenir. The first crowd was pricing a business. The second was pricing a story they wanted to be part of, and pricing it against nothing, because what is the comparable for Mars?

This is Damodaran's oldest distinction, and it carries the whole episode: valuing is estimating what the cash flows justify; pricing is guessing what the next person will pay. Both are real activities. Both can pay. But only one of them has a floor under it, and you find out which crowd you bought from on the days the story wobbles. The past month, with the stock sliding from $161 toward $135 on no rocket-specific news whatsoever, is what it looks like when the souvenir buyers finish buying and the marginal shareholder starts asking spreadsheet questions again.

There's a governance wrinkle that the pricing crowd mostly waved through, and it deserves a paragraph of its own. Elon Musk owns about 42 percent of the economics of SpaceX and controls roughly 80 percent of the votes. Buying SPCX is buying a minority, non-voting seat on a vehicle steered entirely by one man, a man whose defining corporate habit is bolting his ventures together (xAI is now inside the rocket company, a sentence I encourage you to read twice). Damodaran, who has been publicly fond of Musk's companies for years, put it about as gently as the facts permit: hard company to buy, dangerous company to short. Both clauses matter.

The index question

Now the subplot that I think becomes the main plot within a year or two, and it connects to something I wrote last Saturday about who shows up at Treasury auctions.

The S&P 500's rules say a company must be listed for at least a year, with adequate float and four profitable quarters, before it can join the index. SpaceX therefore can't enter before mid-2027 at the earliest, and OpenAI and Anthropic, still private but each flirting with trillion-dollar marks, can't enter at all. There is now open pressure on S&P to bend these rules, and Damodaran's June essay on the fight had a line I haven't stopped thinking about: S&P needs these companies in its index more than they need to be in it. An index claiming to represent American large-cap equity, while excluding two or three of the largest caps in America, has a product problem.

Why does anyone care so much? Because index inclusion is the last remaining source of automatic buying in the entire financial system. The S&P 500 constituents represent something like $63 trillion of float-adjusted market cap, shadowed by trillions of passive dollars that buy whatever the committee blesses, at whatever the price is that day, with the price-sensitivity of a tide. Every 401(k) contribution in a target-date fund is a standing order to purchase the index's members, sight unseen. Getting added is getting plumbed into everyone's paycheck.

A theme is developing in these essays: markets keep replacing discretionary buyers with mechanical ones, and then acting surprised by the consequences. Last week's essay was about the Treasury market losing its price-insensitive buyers. Equities ran the film in the other direction: the price-insensitive buyer took over. When SPCX eventually joins the index, a slug of it goes into every retirement account in the country automatically, no spreadsheet consulted, at 156 times whatever the multiple is that morning.

Worth remembering, while everyone lobbies: the inclusion pop itself is mostly a ghost. Damodaran's review of the evidence finds the bump from joining the S&P has shrunk decade by decade and has "largely disappeared" in recent years. Tesla, the loudest precedent and the closest cousin, joined in December 2020 amid euphoria and then underperformed the index it had just joined. The prize everyone is fighting over may be a trophy that stopped conferring anything years ago, which would make it a perfect emblem of this whole episode.

The number I'd write down

So what's the takeaway, with the stock hovering around its IPO price and the tape ugly?

Write down $110. That's roughly where Damodaran's base-case $1.22 trillion lands per share, and it's the most useful single number in this story, because it was computed before the listing, by someone with no position to defend, using assumptions that already grant SpaceX almost everything. Above it, you are paying for outcomes beyond his most generous line items; at it, you're paying for mere spectacular success; well below it, and the sober spreadsheet says the rocket company is cheap, which is a sentence that rarely stays true for long.

The stock will get its stress test soon enough. The IPO lockup expires in early August, releasing early holders to sell into whatever market August provides, and August is providing wars and a tech unwind. My marker, and you may hold me to it: SPCX trades through $135 and spends time in the low hundreds before Labor Day, at which point the same commentators calling it the people's rocket in June will call it a cautionary tale, and both takes will be exactly as lazy. The company was never worth $1.79 trillion and was never worth $800 billion either, and the distance between those numbers was never about rockets. It was the going rate for a story, quoted twice, to two different crowds.

The rockets, to their credit, keep flying. Rockets are the only part of this that was ever indifferent to the ticker, and there is a lesson in the fact that they are also the only part that works.
