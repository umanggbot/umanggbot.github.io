The Boy Who Was Right

1. The essay

In June 2024, a 22-year-old who had just been fired from OpenAI published a 165-page essay called "Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead." Its claims read, at the time, like science fiction with footnotes: that artificial general intelligence was plausible by 2027; that trillion-dollar compute clusters would be built; that the scarce inputs of the coming economy would be chips, electricity, and secrets; and that almost nobody in power was pricing any of it. The author was Leopold Aschenbrenner. Born in Germany to two physicians, he had entered Columbia at 15, graduated valedictorian at 19 with a professor on record saying his scholarship exceeded any student's in the department in twenty years, joined OpenAI's safety team at 21, and been marched out at 22 over an alleged leak he called a formality of his dissent.

The essay went viral in the way that matters, among the people who allocate money. Within months, Aschenbrenner had done the most 2020s thing imaginable: he turned the manifesto into a hedge fund. Situational Awareness LP would trade the thesis directly. Long the physical substrate of the AI future: memory-chip makers, data-center builders, the power companies that would feed them. Short the doomed: legacy software, the SaaS companies whose products a competent AI would write for free. Sophisticated investors, reportedly including trading firms of the calibre of Jane Street, wired in billions. Standard fees, two and twenty. The pitch was clean: you have read the essay; this is the essay, with a ticker.

For two years, the essay was right, and the fund was rightness converted into numbers. Through 2025 it compounded relentlessly, and in the first half of 2026 it went vertical: returns approaching 400 percent, assets swelling toward $45 billion at the June peak, a 27-year-old... no, he is still only 25... a 25-year-old running one of the fifty largest pools of hedge-fund capital on earth, on a thesis he had written before he could rent a car.

On July 29, the fund sold essentially its entire public book, at a discount, to Citadel, because it had to. Assets today: roughly $10 billion, and the door. Four weeks, top to gone.

2. The trade

To understand what killed it, you need the shape of the portfolio, because the shape is the story.

The longs were the buildout: SanDisk for memory, Nebius for data centers, Bloom Energy for on-site power, and their cousins across the AI-industrial complex. The shorts were the disrupted: a basket of software-as-a-service companies whose moats, the thesis said, were about to be filled in by the very models the longs were fueling. Long the shovel-makers, short the people the gold rush would bury. And across the whole structure: leverage reportedly reaching 400 percent, four dollars of position for every dollar of investor capital.

Notice the elegance, and then notice the flaw hiding inside the elegance. A long-short book like this looks hedged: whatever the market does, you win the spread between the future and the past. That logic holds against market risk. It does not hold against the specific thing that actually happened in July, which was a violent rotation: money gushing out of the crowded AI-infrastructure names (down and down, week after week, as the broader AI unwind that started in June found its second leg) and into, of all things, the beaten-up SaaS sector, the most hated corner of the market, which is to say the most shorted, which is to say the most explosive. His longs fell and his shorts rose. Both blades of the scissors closed on the same hand.

Why would the market do something so seemingly perverse? Because that's what markets under stress do, mechanically, and the mechanism deserves to be kept somewhere you can reach it. When many funds hold similar positions with borrowed money, their portfolios are secretly one portfolio. The moment losses force any of them to shrink (a margin call, a risk limit, a nervous investor), they must sell what everyone owns and buy back what everyone's short. Selling the owned thing pushes it down; buying back the shorted thing pushes it up; both moves inflict losses on every neighbor holding the same shape, forcing them to shrink too. The crowd's own unwind becomes the weather. Prices during such an episode are not voting on any thesis. They are simply reporting whose collateral ran out, in order.

At four turns of leverage, Situational Awareness was not positioned to survive the weather it helped make possible. Run the arithmetic: at 4x, a roughly 12 percent adverse move in your gross book erases half your equity; the moves in July were larger. By the accounts that surfaced within days, the fund's public equities lost about two-thirds of their value in four weeks, the margin calls arrived on schedule, and the book went to Citadel the way books go in these moments: whole, fast, cheap, to the one buyer with the balance sheet and the nerve. (There is always such a buyer, and it is instructive who it always is: the firm that sizes for storms. Morgan in 1907, Citadel in 2026. The fire brigade charges for water; that is why it still exists when the fire comes.)

3. The error

The temptation is to say the kid was wrong, and the temptation should be resisted, because it misses the only lesson worth the tuition.

Look at the components. Was the thesis wrong? The chips-power-and-data buildout has been the defining capital-spending event of the decade; his manifesto's "trillion-dollar cluster" line, mocked in 2024, reads today like a budget line item. Were the shorts wrong? This newsletter wrote three weeks ago about private credit funds gagging on defaulted loans to SaaS companies; the erosion he shorted is literally showing up in other people's collateral. The market has spent the summer disagreeing violently about the price of his future. It has not particularly disagreed about the direction.

What was wrong was something that doesn't appear in the essay, because it never does. Sizing. Conviction answers the question "what happens." Position size answers "when am I required to be right by." Leverage is a time machine that moves your deadline closer: unlevered, you can be early for years (early and solvent is just called patient); at four turns, you must be right this quarter, this month, sometimes this week. Aschenbrenner had a decade-scale thesis financed on week-scale terms. The market did not refute his argument. It simply invoked the clause his leverage had signed, the one that says: your opinion expires before your evidence arrives.

There is even a formula for this, and its existence is the quiet scandal of professional investing. The Kelly criterion, worked out in 1956, computes the maximum fraction of a bankroll you can repeatedly bet, at given odds, before growth turns into guaranteed ruin. Its core finding: bet beyond that fraction and it does not matter how good your edge is; the arithmetic of drawdowns eats you with probability approaching one. Overbetting converts a winning strategy into a losing one, mechanically, without a single bad forecast. A concentrated book at 400 percent leverage in correlated names is so far past any Kelly boundary that the blowup stops being a risk and becomes a scheduled event, with only the date unknown. He was running a strategy whose expected outcome, conditional on enough time passing, was exactly what happened last week.

The pattern is old enough to have grandparents. Long-Term Capital Management, 1998: two Nobel laureates, correct convergence trades, 25-to-1 leverage, dead in a summer; the trades converged and paid, afterward, for other people. The dot-com shorts of 1999 were right about everything and carried out on stretchers in March 2000, weeks before vindication. Archegos, 2021: concentration plus leverage plus speed, $20 billion in days. The market keeps teaching one course with different professors: being right is a forecast; staying solvent is a structure; and the second is senior to the first, the way a mortgage is senior to a mood.

4. The remainder

Two footnotes to the wreck, each carrying its own small essay.

The first: reportedly, one major asset did survive the fire sale untouched. The fund's private stake in Anthropic could not be margin-called, because nothing marks it to market daily; no lender reprices it at 4 p.m.; there is no tape for it to gap down on. The most volatile thesis in the portfolio was housed in the only wrapper volatility couldn't reach. Readers of the private-credit essay will feel the rhyme, from the opposite direction: the same illiquidity that traps investors in gated funds is what saved this position's life. Illiquidity is neither friend nor enemy. It is a wall, and which side of it you're on at the moment of stress is most of what matters.

The second: the money that fled his longs in July went into his shorts, which means, if the manifesto is right on the decade, the crowd has spent the summer buying the disrupted with the proceeds of the future, at speed, for reasons of pure mechanics. Somewhere in that fact is either the trade of 2027 or the next essay's punchline, and I honestly don't know which.

5. The grade

So how should the record book enter him? Here is my attempt at fairness. The thesis: still live, and partially vindicated in real time. The analysis: by every account, brilliant. The structure: an instrument that converted brilliance into ruin on a fixed schedule. Markets do not grade theses; they grade positions, and a position is a thesis plus a size plus a date, and he flunked on the two components nobody writes manifestos about.

He is 25. Columbia's economics department called him the best they'd seen in twenty years, and the worst month of his professional life just handed him the one dataset his essay lacked. The history of finance is unsentimental about blowups but surprisingly generous about second acts; the industry forgives losses far faster than it forgives being boring, and he has never once been boring. I'd give even odds we see the name Situational Awareness on a term sheet again before the manifesto's own 2027 deadline.

The deeper grade lands on the rest of us, the audience that made a $45 billion fund out of a PDF in eighteen months. The lesson we'll take, if history is a guide, is that he was a genius, or that he was a fraud, and both are alibis. The accurate lesson is cheaper and harder: the future being knowable is a different fact from the future being financeable, and the distance between those two facts is measured in margin calls. On that measure, the essay was right, the fund was wrong, and the market, as always, declined to wait for the footnotes.
