The People Who Price a War by Lunchtime

Umang  ·  8 Jul 2026  ·  7 min read

The news this morning is that the ceasefire is dead.

The news this morning is that the ceasefire is dead. Iran fired on three tankers in the Strait of Hormuz yesterday, on the theory that they had failed to comply with what Tehran now calls its control of the passage. Washington reinstated oil sanctions. American aircraft hit Iranian coastal targets at Qeshm, Bandar Abbas, and Sirik overnight; Iran answered with strikes on 85 American-allied sites across Bahrain and Kuwait. The president, asked about the memorandum his own negotiators signed three weeks ago, said "as far as I'm concerned, it's over."

Brent jumped from $72 to $78 by breakfast. Seoul fell more than 5 percent. Tokyo lost 2. So did Mumbai. Four days ago, in this newsletter, I wrote that the futures market had priced the war at zero and that the tarp over it had a date printed on it. I did not expect to be proven right in under a week, and I take no satisfaction in it, partly because people are being shot at, and partly because there was a market that got this far more right than I did, and today's essay is about them.

While the futures strip spent June declaring peace, there was a group of professionals, most of them working within a few hundred meters of each other in the City of London, who never signed off on it. They don't trade oil. They price the ships that carry it, one voyage at a time, and their prices never came back down. This morning, the gap between what they believed and what the oil market believed closed violently, in their favor.

I think they are the most underrated forecasters in finance, and the mechanics of what they do explain more about how the world actually runs than any central bank speech you will read this year.

The clause

Start with a fact that surprises almost everyone who learns it: no navy closed the Strait of Hormuz this spring. No blockade was declared. What closed the strait, in the operational sense that mattered, was an insurance clause.

Nearly every merchant ship on earth carries war-risk insurance, and nearly all of it is written or reinsured through the Lloyd's market in London, which handles an estimated 70 to 80 percent of global marine war cover. Buried in those policies is a mechanism of quiet, absolute power: when underwriters designate a stretch of water as a listed high-risk area, coverage there can be cancelled or repriced on seven days' notice. Not renegotiated at the annual renewal. Seven days. At the peak of the crisis this spring, underwriters stopped quoting fixed terms entirely and moved to pricing Gulf transits day by day, which is the actuarial equivalent of a doctor refusing to give a prognosis beyond tomorrow.

So picture the decision that actually keeps a tanker at anchor. No admiral weighs geopolitics anywhere near it. A shipowner with a $110 million vessel listens to a broker explain what this week's cover costs, and does division.

Before the war, hull war cover for a seven-day Gulf transit ran about 0.25 percent of the vessel's insured value. For a large crude carrier worth $100 to 120 million, call it $250,000 to $300,000 per trip. A real cost, but a rounding error against a cargo of two million barrels.

At the peak this spring, the same seven days of cover reached 1 percent of hull value. One million to $1.2 million. Per transit. Each way.

Do the arithmetic slowly, because this is the part I find beautiful. An insurance premium is a probability wearing a dollar sign: the underwriter's estimate of the chance of losing the ship, times the cost of the ship, plus a margin for being wrong. When the market charges 1 percent of a vessel's value for one week of exposure, it is telling you it puts the odds of catastrophe for that ship, that week, somewhere near one in a hundred. For context, the ordinary total-loss rate in commercial shipping is measured in hundredths of a percent per year. The underwriters were pricing a single lap through Hormuz as roughly a thousand times more dangerous than a normal year at sea.

Nobody at Lloyd's held a press conference to announce this. They just quoted the number, and the number did the governing. Owners who wouldn't pay it anchored their ships outside the Gulf; at the worst point, some 80 million barrels sat queued in and around the strait, waiting for paper. Oil, it turns out, flows at the speed of documents.

There is a strategic insight buried here that the war coverage keeps missing. Iran understood this system perfectly. You do not need to sink many tankers to close the world's most important waterway. You need to sink approximately one, credibly threaten a second, and let the seven-day clauses do the rest. The mines and drones were, in a sense, inputs to an actuarial model in London, and the model was always going to be the thing that shut the door. Insurance is the transmission mechanism between violence and commerce, and it transmits fast.

The two guilds

Which brings me to the disagreement, the one that resolved itself at $78 this morning.

Through late June, the oil futures market unwound the entire war premium. Brent closed at $72.68 on June 25, below where it stood the day before the first strike in February. The strip looked at the ceasefire memorandum, the resumed tanker traffic, the 20 million barrels exiting the strait in a single day, and concluded: over.

The insurance market looked at the same strait and declined to agree. Premiums came off their peaks as traffic resumed, but underwriters kept Hormuz listed, kept the short-notice machinery armed, and kept pricing Gulf transits at multiples of the old baseline. The industry's own explanation, which I'd frame as a philosophy of knowledge disguised as a business practice, is that war-risk pricing normalizes months after the politics, because underwriters do not accept announcements as evidence. They want sustained, boring proof of safety: weeks of vessel-tracking data showing transits completing, cargoes discharging, nothing exploding. A ceasefire, to an underwriter, is a hypothesis. A thousand uneventful voyages are a fact.

Think about why the two guilds diverge. Intelligence has nothing to do with it; the difference is the shape of what they sell. An oil futures trader who is wrong about peace loses a spread; he marks to market every day and can reverse in seconds. An underwriter who is wrong about peace loses the whole ship. One of them can afford to trade the announcement. The other can only afford to trade the evidence. When you want to know what's actually true about a dangerous place, ask the person who has to pay for the hull, whole, on the day it disappears.

Yesterday Iran fired on three tankers, and the underwriters' epistemology won the argument. The strip repriced six months of geopolitics in one session. The war-risk market barely had to move. It had never left.

A few other readings from this morning's tape, quickly, because they'll matter in the coming weeks. Gold fell 2.2 percent, to $4,066, on a morning when missiles were flying, which sounds impossible until you remember that on the worst days investors sell what they can, to cover what they must; the safe asset becomes the ATM. Hold that thought, because gold in 2026 is a stranger story than it looks, and I'll come back to it in a few issues. Bitcoin, which its adherents spent years billing as the ultimate crisis asset, sat at $61,900, having spent the year proving it trades like a leveraged tech stock with opinions. And the sharpest equity losses this morning were nowhere near the Gulf: Seoul, down 5.35 percent, because South Korea imports roughly 70 percent of its crude from the Middle East and runs on the assumption that the strait is a fact of nature rather than a policy.

What to watch instead of the news

The practical takeaway is a habit. For the rest of this war, and honestly for every conflict after it, skip the headlines about ceasefires and read the insurance signals instead. They are public, they are numerical, and they are set by people who are ruinously punished for optimism.

The specific tell: the day hull war cover for a Hormuz transit gets quoted back at 0.25 percent, on fixed terms rather than day rates, the war is over, whatever the communiqués say. Underwriters will get there only when the tracking data forces them to, which means that number cannot be spun, lobbied, or announced into existence. It is the closest thing this conflict has to an honest referee.

We are, for the record, nowhere near it. As of this morning the quotes are moving the other way, the seven-day notices are going out, and the queue outside the strait has started growing again. The last time the underwriters and the futures market disagreed, it took the strip three months and a $47 round trip to catch up to what London already believed.

The bookmakers set the odds. The underwriters decide whether the game gets played at all. This morning they suspended play, quietly, seven days at a time, and my strong advice is to believe them over every printed word out of Washington and Tehran combined, including, obviously, mine.
