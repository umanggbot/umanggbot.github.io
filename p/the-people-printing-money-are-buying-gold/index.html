The People Printing Money Are Buying Gold

Umang  ·  25 Jul 2026  ·  8 min read

On the last weekend of February 2022, while Russian tanks idled outside Kyiv, officials in Washington, Brussels, and Tokyo did something no one had ever done to a great power. They froze the Central Bank of Russia's foreign reserves, roughly $300 billion, and almost none of it sat in any vault in Moscow. It existed as claims on Western institutions: Treasury bonds in custody accounts, deposits at Western banks, euros at Euroclear. Russia had spent two decades building a war chest. It turned out to be parked in the enemy's garage.

Every reserve manager on earth watched that weekend and learned the same lesson, which has nothing to do with Russia and everything to do with what money is. A reserve held in someone else's currency is a promise, and promises have an off switch, and the switch is in someone else's building. There is exactly one reserve asset with no off switch, no counterparty, no custodian who answers to a foreign ministry. It is a shiny yellow rock, and since that weekend, the world's central banks have been buying it at a pace unseen since the 1960s.

I bring this up now because gold is having the strangest year of any major asset, and you cannot understand the strangeness without the Moscow lesson. The strangeness comes in two facts that should not coexist.

Fact one: gold has crashed. After a historic melt-up that carried it from under $2,000 in late 2023 to a peak near $5,600 early this year, it has fallen back to around $4,000. It closed June at $4,018, down 14 percent for the second quarter alone, down more than a quarter from the top. Silver fell 22 percent last quarter. On July 8, the day Iranian missiles hit 85 targets across the Gulf and Brent jumped eight percent, gold went down 2.2 percent. Read that sentence twice. War broke out, and the war asset fell.

Fact two: the biggest, most deliberate buyers on the planet did not blink. On July 7, the People's Bank of China announced its twentieth consecutive monthly increase in gold reserves, adding about 15 tonnes in June, its largest monthly purchase of the current streak. Not despite the falling price. Visibly, cheerfully, into it. Buying the dip, as a matter of state policy.

So the price of gold is collapsing while the most sophisticated and least price-sensitive buyers in the world accelerate their purchases. Someone is selling urgently enough to overwhelm them. Today's essay is about who, and why, and what it tells you about an asset that, I'll argue, most people misread, including many of the people who own it.

Start with the misunderstanding, because everything else hangs off it. Gold produces nothing. No coupons, no dividends, no earnings calls. You cannot do to gold what we did to SpaceX two weeks ago, projecting cash flows and discounting them, because there are no cash flows to discount. An asset with no cash flows cannot be valued. It can only be priced, which means: it is worth what the next person will pay, full stop, and all analysis of gold is analysis of who the next person is.

This sounds like an insult. It's a classification, and it explains gold's behavior better than any inflation model. The question to ask about gold is never "what is it worth" (unanswerable) but "who is the marginal buyer and seller right now" (answerable, and profitable).

And the answer in 2026 is that gold has two almost completely separate markets wearing one price.

The first market is Western and financial. It trades gold: ETFs in London and New York, futures on COMEX, momentum funds, wealth managers sprinkling three percent into portfolios, retail buyers who watched the melt-up on their phones. In January, with the price near its peak, a Kitco survey found 71 percent of retail investors expected gold above $5,000 by year-end. That crowd owns gold on leverage and on feelings, marks it to market daily, and can be forced to sell by a margin clerk. To this market, gold is a trade with a chart.

The second market is Eastern and official. It accumulates gold: the PBoC, the central banks of the Gulf and Central Asia, institutions running the Moscow lesson through their reserve models. This crowd does not mark to market in any way that matters, publishes its purchases with a delay and a shrug, and measures position size in decades. To this market, gold is a fifty-year decision that happens to have a daily price: the only money that cannot be frozen by a Treasury official on a weekend.

Once you see the two markets, the 2026 crash resolves into something much more interesting than a crash. Walk the causes in order.

The melt-up itself was the first market at full sprint: momentum, ETF inflows, the 2025 fear trade around deficits and de-dollarization headlines, each price record generating the next buyer. Assets with no cash flows have no anchor, which means they overshoot in both directions; nothing says "stop" at $5,600 for the same reason nothing said "stop" at $2,000.

Then, over the spring, the first market's reasons evaporated one by one. The new Fed chair arrived in May with a hawkish reputation and a doctrine of less accommodation, and gold's oldest enemy is a credible central banker paying a real yield; every uptick in expected rates raises the cost of holding the asset that pays nothing. Inflation ran hot (3.8 percent CPI, 6 percent producer prices in the spring prints), which textbook logic says should help gold, and instead hurt it, because hot inflation now summons rate hikes, and the hikes hurt more than the inflation helps. The dollar strengthened. The June ceasefire subtracted the war premium. And when the tech selloff hit in late June, gold fell with the Nasdaq, because in a deleveraging, the safe asset is the ATM: you sell what has a bid to cover what doesn't. July 8 was the purest specimen. Missiles flew, margin calls landed, and gold funded them.

Every one of those forces operated on the first market. None of them touched the second. The PBoC does not care about Warsh's dot plot, has no margin clerk, and is executing a two-decade policy at whatever the London fix says today, where a lower fix simply means more tonnes per billion. Twenty months, no pause, biggest purchase of the streak into June's lows.

Which yields the cleanest way to describe 2026's gold market, and the sentence I'd keep from this essay: the crash and the accumulation are the same event, seen from opposite sides. The West's traders are selling gold to the East's states, urgently, at a discount, and the price is set by the urgency of the sellers while the ownership migrates to hands that will never give it back. Metal is leaving the map of things that trade and entering the map of things that sit in vaults for fifty years. Market people call this a bear market. A geologist would call it sequestration.

Does the migration matter? I think it matters more than almost anything else the price could tell you, for a reason that takes one more layer of ELI5.

Ask why a central bank, an institution whose entire product is paper money, hoards the one asset that competes with paper money. The Moscow lesson is the sharp answer, and there's a softer, older one underneath it: a reserve is supposed to be the thing that works when everything else doesn't. Treasuries work as long as Washington is your friend. Euros work as long as Brussels is. Gold works, period; its whole value proposition is indifference. It pays no interest precisely because it makes no promises, and a thing that makes no promises cannot break any. Central banks are not buying an investment. They are buying the absence of a counterparty: insurance against a world where the plumbing itself takes sides, written by the only insurer that never asks questions.

Seen that way, the streak through the crash is not stubbornness. An insurance buyer who stops paying premiums because insurance got cheaper has misunderstood his own program. The PBoC's June purchase at $4,000 and its purchases at $5,400 are the same decision; only the exchange rate between paper and indifference moved.

Now the falsifiable part, because an essay about an unanchored asset owes you markers.

First marker: the war test. The ceasefire is dead, the Gulf is burning again, and the strait's reopening is in doubt. If gold cannot reclaim and hold $4,500 with an actual shooting war running, then the old reflex (war, therefore gold) is finished for this cycle, and the first market's deleveraging still owns the tape. As of this morning gold sits near $4,050, and the reflex is on trial.

Second marker: the streak. Watch the PBoC's monthly announcement, first week of each month, the dullest press release in finance and currently the most informative. The streak surviving a 28 percent drawdown tells you the buyer is insensitive to price. If the streak ever stops on a price spike rather than a price fall, that tells you the insurance program has a budget after all. Twenty months in, no budget is visible.

Third: the arbitrage between the two markets has a tell. Physical premiums in Shanghai over the London price, and central-bank tonnage reported by the World Gold Council each quarter. If Western ETF outflows slow while official buying holds, the price finds its floor at whatever level the states are willing to absorb the West's remaining urgency, and I'd expect that floor to be violent, because insurance buyers with printing presses do not run out of money. They run out of sellers.

There's a version of this essay that ends with a prediction about the price, and I'm declining to write it, for the honest reason that unanchored assets make fools of anyone with a target. I'll end instead with the asymmetry that the whole year has exposed. The people selling gold in 2026 are selling because their charts broke, their margins called, their trades needed funding: reasons measured in days. The people buying are buying because they watched a $300 billion war chest evaporate over a weekend and resolved, at the level of national policy, never to be in that position: reasons measured in generations. Day-traders versus dynasties, and the dynasties are getting filled.

The price will tell you who's winning this week. The tonnage tells you who's winning the century.
