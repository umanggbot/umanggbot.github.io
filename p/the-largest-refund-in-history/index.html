The Largest Refund in History

Umang  ·  22 Jul 2026  ·  8 min read

The largest financial operation of 2026 runs on CSV files.

The largest financial operation of 2026 runs on CSV files.

Somewhere in a Customs and Border Protection system called CAPE, launched on April 20 with the fanfare of a printer-driver update, importers upload spreadsheets listing their customs entries, up to 9,999 rows per declaration, one row per shipment of sneakers or brake pads or garden gnomes. A declaration, once accepted, cannot be amended; get a tax ID wrong and you start over, which about 15 percent of early filers discovered the hard way. Files that clear are queued for payment, by ACH only, paper checks having been abolished in February. And through this contraption, the United States government is currently wiring back the better part of $175 billion in taxes that the Supreme Court says it should never have collected.

In June alone, $49.1 billion went out the door. That is the largest month of refunds in American history. By July 10, the running total hit $86.3 billion, with $121.75 billion of claims accepted for processing and roughly $80 billion still owed. For scale: the government is refunding, per month, more than it spends on NASA per year, and it is doing so with interest, compounded daily.

I want to walk through this machine today for two reasons. The obvious one is that it's a spectacular, under-covered story: the fiscal equivalent of a dam removal, with hedge funds circling and class-action lawyers filing and the Treasury bleeding from a wound inflicted by its own Constitution. The better one is that the refund is accidentally running the greatest economics experiment of the decade. For years, economists argued about who actually paid the Trump tariffs. Importers? Foreign exporters? American consumers? The argument was theoretical. Now $175 billion has to physically land in someone's bank account, and every dollar of it is a data point. We are about to find out who paid, by watching who fights hardest to get paid back.

The machine

Rewind quickly. In February, the Supreme Court decided Learning Resources v. Trump (the lead plaintiff, delightfully, a family-owned educational toy company from Illinois) and struck down the tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act: the "Liberation Day" duties, the fentanyl-justified ones, the reciprocal schedules. The Court's reasoning, compressed: IEEPA lets a president regulate commerce in an emergency, and a permanent, revenue-raising tariff wall is a tax, and taxes belong to Congress. Whatever your politics, note what the ruling did fiscally. Roughly $164.7 billion had been collected under IEEPA authority by January. That money was now, legally speaking, not the government's.

Governments have owed money before. What makes this episode strange is the mechanics of owing it back to hundreds of thousands of importers, entry by entry, with statutory interest running at 6 percent for corporations and 7 percent for individuals, compounded daily, under a customs regulation written for ordinary billing disputes. Compounded daily is not a phrase that usually appears next to eleven-figure sums. While the refunds crawl, the meter runs at junk-bond rates. The federal government has spent 2026 as the most creditworthy borrower on earth paying some of the worst rates in America, on a loan it never meant to take, from lenders who never meant to make it.

And the crawl matters, because the machine has a sequencing problem that will surprise no one who has ever dealt with a large institution. The first, cleanest payments went to the biggest, best-lawyered filers; Walmart and Ford were reportedly among the early recipients, their entries filed clean, their banking details in order. Meanwhile roughly 70 percent of smaller importers are still waiting, thousands of approved claims are stuck on invalid bank details, and an $11.4 billion pile of older entries waits for a later phase of the system. This month the Court of International Trade added a twist with an order whose practical meaning, per the trade bar, is that importers who actually sued get made whole fastest, while everyone who politely waited stands in the administrative queue. The government, for its part, is appealing the refund order even as it pays, a posture best described as writing checks while arguing you shouldn't have to.

If you want a one-line summary of the machine: the money is flowing, the interest is compounding, and the speed of your justice is a function of the quality of your paperwork.

The market

Now the fun part. Wherever there is a large pot of money arriving at an uncertain date, finance will manufacture a way to sell the waiting.

Within weeks of the ruling, a secondary market in tariff refund claims had formed, estimated as large as $100 billion in face value. The trade is simple. You are an importer owed $20 million someday. A fund offers you cash now for the claim, or a loan against it at about 50 cents of advance per dollar of claim, minimum size $10 million, interest accruing in kind. The buyers are hedge funds and, inevitably, private credit funds, an industry currently gating its own investors (see Saturday's essay) while raising money to buy receivables from the United States government.

The pricing history of these claims is the part I'd frame and hang on a wall. Before the Supreme Court ruled, refund claims changed hands at around 20 cents on the dollar. After the ruling: up to 60 cents. Which means that before February, the sharpest money in the country was pricing the odds that the Supreme Court would strike down the tariffs at something like one in five, net of waiting costs, and you could trade that opinion. Constitutional law had a price, updated continuously, posted in a market almost nobody knew existed. The law schools teach doctrine. The claims desks were running a prediction market on it, with nine-figure stakes.

Why only 60 cents even now, with the government literally paying? Because the fine print is a minefield, and the fine print is where this story gets almost baroquely good. A Civil War-era statute, the Assignment of Claims Act, makes selling a claim against the government legally awkward until the claim is formally allowed and warranted, so the funds structure around it with liens and proceeds interests of contested strength. If the importer goes bankrupt before its customs entries are reliquidated, the refund might legally be born after the bankruptcy filing, potentially cutting the lender out entirely; no court has yet decided when, exactly, a tariff refund becomes property. Some importers' own credit agreements contain sweep clauses that grab extraordinary receipts and force them into debt prepayment, meaning the refund arrives and immediately leaves for the benefit of a lender who read the documents more carefully than anyone else. Sixty cents is what certainty about the government's debt looks like after subtracting uncertainty about everything else.

Who paid

Which brings us to the deepest layer, the one that turns this from a plumbing story into an economics experiment.

The refunds go to importers, because importers are who legally paid. Cue the obvious scream: wait, the importers passed the tariff on to us. And so, right on schedule, consumer class actions have been filed against refund-collecting importers, on theories of unjust enrichment, arguing the companies raised prices to cover the tariffs and would now be paid twice. Business customers are combing their supply contracts for surcharge and change-in-law clauses to claw back their share. The trade bar is openly advising claim buyers that the gross refund from CBP "may not equal the net value retained," which is lawyer for: expect to share.

The fight underneath rests on a concept worth more than most finance degrees, so let's have it in plain terms. Who pays a tax has two answers: the legal one (who writes the check) and the economic one (whose prices and wages end up worse). They are almost never the same person. Put a levy on importers and the importer writes the check, then tries to recover it: charge customers more, squeeze the foreign supplier for a discount, eat thinner margins. How the burden splits depends on who can least afford to walk away, and economists studying the 2018-2025 tariffs found, over and over, that foreign exporters barely budged their prices, which means the burden landed almost entirely inside America, split between company margins and consumer prices, in proportions that vary by product and remain fought over in the journals.

The refund now has to run that film backwards. The check goes to the legal payer. The economic payers, armed with subpoenas, want their share back out of it. Which means American courtrooms are about to spend years computing, product by product, invoice by invoice, the very pass-through rates that the economics profession could only estimate in aggregate. The tariff, in death, will produce better data on tariff incidence than it ever did in life. Somewhere, a graduate student is going to build a career on the discovery documents.

Step back and look at the fiscal picture, because it connects to everything else this newsletter has been tracking. The deficit was already tracking past $2 trillion this year. Subtract the tariff revenue that vanished with the ruling (IEEPA duties were more than half of all customs collections in January), add $175 billion of refunds with daily-compounding interest, add a war's worth of spending, and you begin to understand why the fiscal argument has stopped being a think-tank pastime and started showing up in bond auctions. The Treasury is simultaneously refunding a tax, fighting the refund on appeal, and borrowing to fund both the refund and the fight. On the country's 250th birthday I wrote that the auctions define "excessive" one Tuesday at a time. Add this: sometimes the Supreme Court gets a vote too.

The marker I'll put down: the refund story's second act is nastier than its first. Act one was mechanical (file the CSV, receive the wire). Act two is distributional, and distribution fights are where the knives come out: importer versus customer, claim-buyer versus bankruptcy trustee, the government versus its own obligation, appeal pending, interest compounding daily in the background like a taxi meter nobody can find the off switch for. By my arithmetic the interest alone on the unpaid balance is accruing at something like $13 million a day. The toy company won. The lawyers, as ever, won bigger. And the question the whole episode leaves standing is the one it started with, now with empirical stakes: when a government taxes imports, who truly pays? The answer, being assembled right now in a thousand dockets: everyone, in shares to be determined, with interest.
